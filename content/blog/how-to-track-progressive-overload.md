Here is how to track progressive overload: for every working set, write down the exercise, the weight, and the reps. Before each session, look at what you did last time on that exercise and aim to beat it by a small amount. A rep, a little weight, or one more set all count. If you record the same four things every set and compare like with like, the trend shows up on its own within a few weeks.

The rest of this guide is the detail. It covers what progressive overload actually is, which variables you can push, exactly what to write down, how to read the log week to week, and what to do when the numbers stop going up.

## What progressive overload actually means

Progressive overload means gradually asking your muscles to do more than they are used to. Your body adapts to the training you give it. Once it has adapted, the same workout stops being a strong signal to get bigger or stronger, so you have to raise the demand a little at a time.

The key word is **gradually**. Overload is not about crushing yourself every session. It is about small, repeatable increases that you can keep making for months. A lifter who adds 2.5 kg (5 lb) to their squat every week for 12 weeks has gained 30 kg (60 lb) of working weight. A lifter who jumps 10 kg at a time usually stalls in three weeks.

The American College of Sports Medicine's [position stand on progression models in resistance training](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/) describes the same idea in formal terms: once you can do one or two reps more than your target with a given load, increase the load by roughly 2 to 10 percent. That is a useful rule of thumb, and it only works if you know what you lifted last time.

### Why you have to track it

You cannot overload what you cannot remember. Most people walk into the gym and guess: "I think I did 70 on bench last week, maybe for 8?" Guessing leads to two problems.

1. **You repeat the same workout for months.** Without a record, you default to the weight that feels familiar. Familiar weights stop producing progress.
2. **You jump too fast.** On a good day you load up far more than last time, miss reps, and then back off for weeks.

A log fixes both. It turns "I feel stronger" into "I did 70 kg for 8, 8, 7 last Tuesday, so today I want 8, 8, 8." That is the whole game.

## The variables you can overload

Weight on the bar is the obvious lever, but it is not the only one. Knowing the full list helps you keep progressing when adding weight is not possible.

| Variable | What changes | Example (kg) | Example (lb) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Load** | More weight, same reps | 60 kg x 8 to 62.5 kg x 8 | 135 lb x 8 to 140 lb x 8 |
| **Reps** | More reps, same weight | 60 kg x 8 to 60 kg x 9 | 135 lb x 8 to 135 lb x 9 |
| **Sets** | More sets, same weight and reps | 3 x 8 to 4 x 8 | 3 x 8 to 4 x 8 |
| **Volume** | Total sets x reps x weight goes up | 1,440 kg to 1,560 kg | 3,240 lb to 3,510 lb |
| **Density** | Same work in less time | 3 min rest to 2 min rest | 3 min rest to 2 min rest |
| **Technique** | Same numbers, better execution | Full depth, controlled lowering | Full depth, controlled lowering |

### Load

Adding weight is the clearest signal of progress, and for compound lifts it is usually the main goal. The challenge is that the smallest jump available may be a large percentage of the weight. Adding 2.5 kg to a 100 kg deadlift is 2.5 percent. Adding 2 kg to a 10 kg dumbbell lateral raise is 20 percent. That is why load is not the only variable worth tracking.

### Reps

Adding reps is the easiest way to progress when the next weight jump is too big. Going from 8 reps to 10 reps at the same weight is real progress, and it builds you toward the next load increase. This is the foundation of [double progression](/blog/double-progression), which is one of the most reliable systems for intermediate lifters.

### Sets

Adding a set raises the total amount of work. It is a useful lever over a training block, but you cannot add sets forever. Most people run out of time and recovery long before they run out of sets to add. Use it sparingly and on purpose.

### Volume

Volume is usually calculated as **sets x reps x weight**. Three sets of 8 at 60 kg is 3 x 8 x 60 = 1,440 kg. In pounds, 3 x 8 x 135 = 3,240 lb. Volume is a handy summary number because it captures load, reps, and sets in one figure.

There is research support for paying attention to it. A 2017 meta-analysis by Schoenfeld and colleagues found a [graded dose-response relationship between weekly training volume and muscle growth](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/): more weekly sets were associated with more hypertrophy. That does not mean "more is always better," but it does mean the amount of work you do matters and is worth tracking.

### Density

Density is how much work you do per unit of time. Doing the same sets with shorter rest is a form of overload. It is the least useful lever for strength, because shorter rest often costs reps on heavy compound lifts. It can be useful on isolation work or when you are short on time. For more on this trade-off, see our guide on [rest between sets for compound vs isolation exercises](/blog/rest-between-sets-compound-vs-isolation).

### Technique

Lifting the same weight with a fuller range of motion, a slower lowering phase, or less body English is harder, even if the numbers look the same. Technique improvements are real progress, but they are hard to record as a number. The safest approach is to set a technique standard for each lift and only count reps that meet it.

## How to track progressive overload: what to record every set

Here is the minimum record that makes progressive overload trackable. You need these four things for every working set.

1. **Exercise name.** Be specific and consistent. "Incline dumbbell press" and "incline DB bench" should not be two different entries in your log.
2. **Weight.** In the unit you actually load. If you use kg plates, log kg. If your gym is in pounds, log pounds. Do not convert in your head mid-session.
3. **Reps completed.** The number you actually did, not the number you planned. If you planned 8 and got 6, write 6.
4. **Set order.** Set 1, set 2, set 3. Fatigue builds across sets, so set 1 and set 3 are not directly comparable.

And for every session, add:

- **Date.** So you can see how often you are training each lift and spot gaps.
- **Session or template name.** "Push A" or "Upper 1," so you compare the right sessions with each other.

### Optional extras

These are helpful but not essential. Add them only if you will actually use them.

- **Rest time** between sets, if you are trying to control density.
- **Body weight**, recorded a few times a week, so you can link strength changes to a bulk or cut. Our guide on [how often to weigh yourself](/blog/how-often-to-weigh-yourself) covers how to do this without obsessing.
- **A short comment** on anything unusual, like poor sleep or a sore shoulder, if your log has room for it.

### Logging dumbbells and bodyweight lifts

Two cases cause confusion.

**Dumbbells:** decide once whether you log the weight per hand or the total of both, then never switch. Most lifters log per hand, because that is what is written on the dumbbell. Our article on [whether to log dumbbells per hand or total](/blog/log-dumbbells-per-hand-or-total) goes through the pros and cons.

**Bodyweight lifts** like pull-ups and dips: log reps as the main number. If you add weight with a belt, log the added weight only (for example, +10 kg or +20 lb) and keep that convention consistent.

### Do you log warm-up sets?

Warm-ups help you prepare, but they should not be compared with working sets. The simplest rule is to log only your working sets. If you want to record warm-ups too, keep them clearly separate so they do not pollute your volume numbers and progress charts.

## A sample progressive overload log

Here is what a clean progressive overload log looks like for one exercise over four weeks. This lifter is doing barbell bench press, 3 sets, aiming for 6 to 8 reps.

### Kilograms

| Week | Set 1 | Set 2 | Set 3 | Volume | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 70 kg x 8 | 70 kg x 7 | 70 kg x 6 | 1,470 kg | Baseline |
| 2 | 70 kg x 8 | 70 kg x 8 | 70 kg x 7 | 1,610 kg | +2 reps |
| 3 | 70 kg x 8 | 70 kg x 8 | 70 kg x 8 | 1,680 kg | Top of range, add load |
| 4 | 72.5 kg x 7 | 72.5 kg x 6 | 72.5 kg x 6 | 1,377.5 kg | New load |

### Pounds

| Week | Set 1 | Set 2 | Set 3 | Volume | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 155 lb x 8 | 155 lb x 7 | 155 lb x 6 | 3,255 lb | Baseline |
| 2 | 155 lb x 8 | 155 lb x 8 | 155 lb x 7 | 3,565 lb | +2 reps |
| 3 | 155 lb x 8 | 155 lb x 8 | 155 lb x 8 | 3,720 lb | Top of range, add load |
| 4 | 160 lb x 7 | 160 lb x 6 | 160 lb x 6 | 3,040 lb | New load |

Notice that volume drops in week 4. That is normal and not a sign of failure. When the load goes up, reps usually drop for a while. The lifter is now working toward 8, 8, 8 at the heavier weight, and the cycle repeats.

## How to read your log week to week

Collecting numbers is the easy part. The value comes from reading them correctly. Here is a simple routine for tracking lifts over time.

### Before each session: check last time

Before you start an exercise, look at exactly what you did in the previous matching session. Then set one target for today:

- If you hit the top of your rep range on every set, **add the smallest available weight**.
- If you did not, **keep the weight and aim for one more rep** on at least one set.
- If your reps dropped noticeably last time, **repeat the same target** and focus on sleep, food, and rest between sets.

That is a decision you can make in five seconds. It is also the single most useful habit for anyone learning how to track progressive overload.

### Every week: compare like with like

Compare the same exercise, in the same session type, at the same position in the workout. Bench press at the start of Push A is not comparable with bench press at the end of a long upper-body day. Order and fatigue change the numbers.

Also compare the **same set number**. Set 1 this week vs set 1 last week tells you more than mixing sets together.

### Every month: look at the trend, not the day

Individual sessions are noisy. Sleep, stress, food, and even the time of day move your numbers by a few percent. That is why tracking lifts over time beats judging any single workout.

A useful monthly check is to look at your best set on each main lift over the last four weeks. You can use the heaviest weight lifted for your target reps, or the most reps at a fixed weight. If that number is flat or rising slowly, you are progressing. If it has been flat for three or more weeks, you have a plateau, which is covered below.

A per-exercise chart makes this much easier than scanning rows of numbers. GymLedger, for example, draws a strength progress chart for each exercise and records your personal bests, so a month of training reads as one line instead of 12 tables.

## How fast should you progress? Beginner vs intermediate

Progress rates slow down as you get more experienced. Setting the right expectation stops you from either coasting or forcing jumps your body cannot handle.

| Stage | Typical progression | What it looks like in the log |
|---|---|---|
| **Beginner** (first 3 to 9 months of consistent lifting) | Load up most sessions on main lifts | Squat 60 kg, 62.5 kg, 65 kg across three sessions (135 lb, 140 lb, 145 lb) |
| **Early intermediate** | Load up weekly or every other week | Rep gains within a range, then a small load jump |
| **Intermediate** | Load up over several weeks | Best sets inch up across a 4 to 8 week block |
| **Advanced** | Progress measured over months | Small PRs at the end of planned training blocks |

These are rough patterns, not rules. Your age, sleep, food, and training history all shift the timeline.

### Beginners: add weight, keep it simple

As a beginner, you can usually add weight to compound lifts often. A simple approach is to pick a fixed sets-and-reps target, such as 3 sets of 5 or 3 sets of 8, and add the smallest jump each time you hit every rep with good form.

For barbell lifts, that is usually **2.5 kg** (two 1.25 kg plates) or **5 lb** (two 2.5 lb plates). For deadlifts and squats early on, some beginners add 5 kg (10 lb) per session for the first few weeks. Upper-body pressing slows down sooner, so keep those jumps small.

If you are just starting, a structured plan helps. Our [12-week gym workout plan for beginners](/blog/gym-workout-plan-for-beginners-india) builds this progression in from day one. If you train in a kg gym and plate math still trips you up, see [how to load a barbell in kg](/blog/how-to-load-a-barbell-in-kg).

### Intermediates: switch to rep ranges

At some point, adding weight every session stops working. You will miss reps, grind, and stall. That is the sign to stop chasing load every session and start using rep ranges.

With a rep range, you keep the weight the same and add reps until you hit the top of the range on all sets. Then you add weight and drop back to the bottom. This is double progression, and it is the system most intermediates should run. It is so useful we wrote a [complete guide to double progression](/blog/double-progression) with multi-week examples.

## Plateaus: how to spot one and what to do

A plateau is when your best set on a lift has not improved for three or more matching sessions, despite good effort. One bad day is not a plateau. Three flat weeks is.

### Check the basics first

Before you change your program, rule out the boring causes. They explain most plateaus.

- **Sleep.** Are you consistently short on sleep?
- **Food.** If you are eating in a deficit, maintaining strength is a win. Expecting steady PRs on a cut is unrealistic for most people.
- **Consistency.** Have you actually trained this lift as planned? A log makes missed sessions obvious.
- **Rest between sets.** Are you rushing compound lifts with 60 seconds of rest? That alone can cost you reps.

### Then adjust one variable

If the basics are fine, change one thing at a time so you can see what worked.

1. **Use smaller jumps.** If your gym has 1.25 kg (2.5 lb) plates or smaller, use them. Microloading lets you keep adding load where a bigger jump would stall.
2. **Switch to a rep range.** If you were doing a fixed 3 x 5, move to 3 sets of 4 to 6 and progress reps first.
3. **Add a set.** Going from 3 to 4 working sets for a few weeks can restart progress, as long as you can recover from it.
4. **Change the exercise variation.** Swap flat bench for incline, or back squat for front squat, for a training block. Track the new lift from a fresh baseline.
5. **Take a deload.** Sometimes fatigue is hiding your progress. More on that next.

## Deloads: planned backing off

A deload is a short, planned period of easier training, usually one week. The goal is to let fatigue fall so your strength can show up again. It is not a week off and it is not a sign of weakness.

### When to deload

There is no universal schedule. Common approaches are:

- **On a schedule**, for example every 6 to 8 weeks of hard training.
- **On signals**, such as reps dropping across several lifts at once, persistent joint aches, or poor sleep despite good habits.

Your log is the best signal. If several lifts drop at the same time, that is usually fatigue, not lost strength.

### How to deload

Pick one approach and log it like any other week.

| Method | What to do | Example (kg) | Example (lb) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cut sets** | Keep weight, do about half the sets | 3 x 8 at 70 kg becomes 2 x 8 at 70 kg | 3 x 8 at 155 lb becomes 2 x 8 at 155 lb |
| **Cut load** | Keep sets, drop weight about 10 to 20 percent | 70 kg becomes 60 kg | 155 lb becomes 135 lb |
| **Cut both** | Fewer sets and lighter weight | 2 x 8 at 60 kg | 2 x 8 at 135 lb |

Log your deload week so it does not confuse your charts. When you look back, a dip in volume with a clear reason is fine. A dip you cannot explain is worth investigating.

After a deload, return to roughly the weights you were using before, not heavier. Most lifters find the first week back feels easier, and progress resumes from there.

## Paper vs spreadsheet vs app

Any method that captures exercise, weight, reps, and date will work. The best tool is the one you will use every single session. Here is how the three main options compare.

| Factor | Paper notebook | Spreadsheet | Workout tracker app |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Speed between sets** | Fast once set up | Slow on a phone | Fast, built for it |
| **Finding last session** | Flip back pages | Scroll or filter | Usually shown for you |
| **Volume and trends** | Manual math | Formulas you build | Usually automatic |
| **Charts** | None | Possible with setup | Usually built in |
| **Backup** | Easy to lose | Cloud if you set it up | Often syncs |
| **Cost** | Cheapest | Free | Free to paid |
| **Privacy** | Fully private | Depends on service | Depends on app |

### Paper notebook

Paper is simple, private, and never runs out of battery. It works well for beginners running a fixed program. The weaknesses are finding old data and doing math. Calculating weekly volume across five exercises by hand gets old quickly, and you will not see a trend unless you draw it.

### Spreadsheet

A spreadsheet is powerful if you enjoy building it. You can calculate volume, make charts, and model your next session. The downside is that spreadsheets are awkward to edit on a phone between sets, and a single broken formula can quietly corrupt months of data.

### Progressive overload tracker app

A progressive overload tracker app is the lowest-effort option for most people. You enter the weight and reps, and the app handles dates, history, and charts. The trade-offs are cost, and that some apps add social feeds, ads, or data sharing you may not want. [GymLedger](/) is one example built around this workflow. You set up templates with your exercises, sets, and rep ranges, log each set's weight and reps quickly, and see per-exercise progress charts and personal bests. It supports kg or lbs and has no ads. Whatever you choose, judge it by one question: does it make it easy to see exactly what you did last time?

## Worked example: a full week in kg and lb

Here is how one lifter might track a two-lift session using a rep range of 6 to 8 on squats and 8 to 12 on leg press. The rule: when all sets reach the top of the range, add the smallest load jump next session.

### Squat: 3 sets, 6 to 8 reps

| Session | kg log | lb log | Decision for next session |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 80 x 8, 8, 7 | 175 x 8, 8, 7 | Same weight, get 8 on set 3 |
| 2 | 80 x 8, 8, 8 | 175 x 8, 8, 8 | All sets at 8: add load |
| 3 | 82.5 x 7, 7, 6 | 180 x 7, 7, 6 | Same weight, build reps |
| 4 | 82.5 x 8, 7, 7 | 180 x 8, 7, 7 | Same weight, build reps |

### Leg press: 3 sets, 8 to 12 reps

| Session | kg log | lb log | Decision for next session |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 140 x 12, 11, 10 | 310 x 12, 11, 10 | Same weight |
| 2 | 140 x 12, 12, 11 | 310 x 12, 12, 11 | Same weight |
| 3 | 140 x 12, 12, 12 | 310 x 12, 12, 12 | All at 12: add 10 kg / 20 lb |
| 4 | 150 x 10, 9, 9 | 330 x 10, 9, 9 | Build reps |

Two things to notice. First, the leg press uses a bigger load jump because it is a machine with a large weight stack and a stronger movement. Second, every decision comes straight from the previous row. There is no guessing.

### Calculating volume in both units

For session 2 of the squat:

- **kg:** 80 x 8 + 80 x 8 + 80 x 8 = 1,920 kg
- **lb:** 175 x 8 + 175 x 8 + 175 x 8 = 4,200 lb

For session 3, after the load jump:

- **kg:** 82.5 x 7 + 82.5 x 7 + 82.5 x 6 = 1,650 kg
- **lb:** 180 x 7 + 180 x 7 + 180 x 6 = 3,600 lb

Volume dropped after the jump, just like in the bench example. Within two or three sessions it typically climbs back above the old number at the new weight. That is the pattern you want to see in your log: a sawtooth that trends up over time.

## Common mistakes when tracking progressive overload

Most tracking problems are not about effort. They come from inconsistent records that make honest comparison impossible.

### 1. Changing exercise names

If "lat pulldown," "wide-grip pulldown," and "pulldown" all appear in your log, you have three short histories instead of one long one. Pick a name and stick with it. Create a separate entry only when the movement actually changes.

### 2. Mixing units

Logging some sessions in kg and others in lb, or switching between per-hand and total on dumbbells, breaks your charts. Choose your units and conventions once. If you change gyms and units, start a clean baseline.

### 3. Counting bad reps

If a rep needed a big bounce, a half range of motion, or a spotter's help, it does not count as the same rep as a clean one. Inflated numbers feel good for a week, then cause a stall when the next jump is impossible.

### 4. Adding weight too fast

Big jumps feel productive and cost you weeks. Use the smallest increment available and let reps tell you when to move up. Slow and steady keeps working.

### 5. Changing too many things at once

New exercise order, new rep range, new rest times, and a new split in the same week means you cannot tell what helped. Change one variable, track it for a few weeks, then decide.

### 6. Ignoring missed sessions

Life happens. What matters is how you handle it. Do not try to cram two workouts into one. Our guide on [what to do if you miss a day on push/pull/legs](/blog/missed-a-day-push-pull-legs) covers how to get back on track without wrecking your progression.

### 7. Only tracking the top set

If you only write down your heaviest set, you miss what is happening to the rest of the workout. Your back-off sets often show fatigue or progress before your top set does. Log every working set.

### 8. Not looking at the log

The most common mistake of all. A log you never read is a diary, not a training tool. Look at last session before every exercise. That five-second habit is most of the benefit.

## Putting it into practice

Here is the simplest version of how to track progressive overload, ready for your next workout.

1. **Pick your tool.** Paper, spreadsheet, or an app like GymLedger. Choose the one you will use every session.
2. **Set rep ranges.** For example, 5 to 8 for heavy compound lifts, 8 to 12 for most accessories, and 10 to 15 for small isolation exercises.
3. **Record every working set.** Exercise, weight, reps, set order, and date. Nothing more is required.
4. **Before each exercise, check last time.** Add weight if you hit the top of the range on all sets. Otherwise, aim for one more rep.
5. **Review monthly.** Look at the trend on each main lift. Flat for three or more weeks means it is time to address a plateau.
6. **Deload when fatigue builds.** Plan one easier week when your log shows several lifts dropping together.

If you want a program structure to apply this to, our guide to [building your own workout split](/blog/how-to-build-your-own-workout-split) walks through choosing days, exercises, and set targets. Then let your log do the deciding.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the easiest way to track progressive overload?

The easiest way is to record the exercise, weight, reps, and date for every working set, then check the previous matching session before each exercise. If you hit the top of your rep range on every set, add a small amount of weight. If not, aim for one more rep. A notebook works, but a workout tracker app saves time because it keeps your history and draws progress charts automatically.

### How often should I increase weight for progressive overload?

It depends on your experience. Beginners can often add weight to main lifts every session or every week. Intermediates usually progress reps within a range first and add weight every few weeks. A common guideline is to increase the load once you can complete one or two reps beyond your target. Use the smallest available increment, such as 2.5 kg or 5 lb on a barbell.

### Is adding reps considered progressive overload?

Yes. Doing more reps with the same weight increases the demand on your muscles, so it counts as progressive overload. It is often the best option when the next weight jump is too big, such as with dumbbells or machines. Adding reps within a set range and then adding weight when you reach the top of the range is called double progression, a reliable method for intermediate lifters.

### Should I track volume or just weight and reps?

Track weight and reps for every set, because volume is calculated from them. Volume, which is sets multiplied by reps multiplied by weight, is a useful summary for comparing sessions and training blocks. Do not panic if volume drops after you add weight. That is expected, and it usually recovers within a few sessions. Focus on the trend over several weeks rather than any single workout.

### How long does a plateau need to last before I change my program?

A reasonable rule is three or more matching sessions with no improvement in your best set, despite good effort. Before changing your program, check sleep, food intake, rest between sets, and consistency, since these explain most stalls. If those are fine, change one variable at a time, such as using smaller jumps, switching to a rep range, adding a set, or taking a deload week.

### Do I need an app to track progressive overload?

No. Any method that records exercise, weight, reps, and date will work, including a paper notebook or spreadsheet. An app mainly saves time and effort. It keeps your history searchable, shows what you did last session, and builds charts so you can see trends without manual math. Choose whichever method you will actually use every session, because consistent records matter more than the tool.
