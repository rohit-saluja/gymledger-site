A simple progressive overload bench press plan looks like this: bench 3 sets of 5 at a weight you can do with good form, and every time you get all 15 reps, add 2.5 kg (5 lb) next session. When that stops working, add smaller amounts of weight, or add reps before weight. The same logic runs every example below, from squats to dumbbells to push-ups. Only the size of the steps changes.

This post is the worked-examples companion to our pillar guide on [how to track progressive overload](/blog/how-to-track-progressive-overload). That guide covers what to write down. This one shows what the numbers look like, week by week, in kg and lb, including the weeks where it goes wrong.

## What an example of progressive overload looks like

Progressive overload means doing a little more than last time. "More" can mean any of these:

| Lever | Example in kg | Example in lb |
|---|---|---|
| **Load** | Bench 60 kg x 5 becomes 62.5 kg x 5 | Bench 135 lb x 5 becomes 140 lb x 5 |
| **Reps** | Row 30 kg x 8 becomes 30 kg x 9 | Row 65 lb x 8 becomes 65 lb x 9 |
| **Sets** | Squat 3 x 6 becomes 4 x 6 at 100 kg | Squat 3 x 6 becomes 4 x 6 at 225 lb |
| **Range or difficulty** | Push-ups on the floor become deficit push-ups | Same |
| **Tempo** | 3-second lowering at the same weight | Same |

Load gets the attention, but reps work too. In an eight-week study of trained lifters, [one group added load and the other added reps at a fixed load](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36199287/). Muscle growth was similar between the groups, and the load group had a small, uncertain edge in squat strength. So when the next plate is too big a jump, adding a rep is still progress.

Every example below follows one rule: **beat the log by the smallest step that still counts**, whether that's one more rep, one small plate or one more set.

## Progressive overload bench press: three examples

Bench press stalls sooner than squats and deadlifts, so it's the lift where step size matters most. Here are three ways to run it, from first month to a few years in.

### Example 1: beginner bench, add weight every session

This is the classic first progressive overload bench press plan: three sets of 5, twice a week. Add 2.5 kg (5 lb) whenever all 15 reps happen with clean form. Repeat the weight if any set falls short.

| Session | kg | lb | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 50 x 5, 5, 5 | 110 x 5, 5, 5 | Add 2.5 kg / 5 lb |
| 2 | 52.5 x 5, 5, 5 | 115 x 5, 5, 5 | Add |
| 3 | 55 x 5, 5, 5 | 120 x 5, 5, 5 | Add |
| 4 | 57.5 x 5, 5, 5 | 125 x 5, 5, 5 | Add |
| 5 | 60 x 5, 5, 4 | 130 x 5, 5, 4 | Repeat |
| 6 | 60 x 5, 5, 5 | 130 x 5, 5, 5 | Add |
| 7 | 62.5 x 5, 4, 4 | 135 x 5, 4, 4 | Repeat |
| 8 | 62.5 x 5, 5, 4 | 135 x 5, 5, 4 | Repeat |
| 9 | 62.5 x 5, 4, 3 | 135 x 5, 4, 3 | Change the plan |

That's 12.5 kg (25 lb) on the bar in about a month. Session 9 is the signal that adding a full 2.5 kg (5 lb) every time has run its course. Two misses in a row at the same weight means it's time for Example 2, not a fourth attempt with louder music.

### Example 2: intermediate bench with microplates

Once session-to-session jumps stall, make the jumps smaller. Here the lifter benches heavy once a week and adds 1 kg (two 0.5 kg plates) or 2.5 lb (two 1.25 lb plates) when all sets are complete.

| Week | kg | lb | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 80 x 5, 5, 5 | 175 x 5, 5, 5 | Add 1 kg / 2.5 lb |
| 2 | 81 x 5, 5, 5 | 177.5 x 5, 5, 5 | Add |
| 3 | 82 x 5, 5, 4 | 180 x 5, 5, 4 | Repeat |
| 4 | 82 x 5, 5, 5 | 180 x 5, 5, 5 | Add |
| 5 | 83 x 5, 5, 5 | 182.5 x 5, 5, 5 | Add |
| 6 | 84 x 5, 4, 4 | 185 x 5, 4, 4 | Repeat |
| 7 | 84 x 5, 5, 5 | 185 x 5, 5, 5 | Add |
| 8 | 85 x 5, 5, 4 | 187.5 x 5, 5, 4 | Repeat |

Five kilograms in eight weeks doesn't sound like much, but it beats benching the same number every January.

No microplates at your gym? Add reps instead. Go from 3 x 5 to 3 x 6 to 3 x 7 at the same weight, then add 2.5 kg (5 lb) and drop back to 5s. That's [double progression](/blog/double-progression), and the full guide has an eight-week bench example on a 6 to 10 rep range.

### Example 3: top set plus back-off sets

A common setup for lifters a few years in: one heavy set of 3, then three lighter sets of 8 at about 80 percent of the top set. Progress the top set first, and let the back-off weight follow it.

| Week | Top set (kg) | Back-off (kg) | Top set (lb) | Back-off (lb) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 90 x 3 | 72.5 x 8, 8, 8 | 200 x 3 | 160 x 8, 8, 8 |
| 2 | 92.5 x 3 | 75 x 8, 8, 8 | 205 x 3 | 165 x 8, 8, 8 |
| 3 | 92.5 x 3 | 75 x 8, 8, 7 | 205 x 3 | 165 x 8, 8, 7 |
| 4 | 95 x 3 | 75 x 8, 8, 8 | 210 x 3 | 170 x 8, 8, 8 |

The trick is deciding when the top set has earned a jump. Many lifters rate how hard it felt on a 1 to 10 scale: if the set of 3 felt like it had two reps left, add weight next week. If it was a grinder, repeat. Our [RPE chart](/blog/rpe-chart) explains the scale. You can rate it in your head or jot it on paper; the weights and reps are what your log needs.

## Progressive overload for squats: two examples

Squats usually climb faster than bench at first, because the legs are big and the starting weights are often light.

![Lifter sliding a weight plate onto a barbell in a power rack](/blog/progressive-overload-examples/loading-barbell.webp)
*Photo: [Juho Alonen](https://www.pexels.com/photo/man-adjusting-barbell-weights-in-gym-28636774/) on Pexels*

### Example 4: beginner squat, three times a week

Three sets of 5, three full-body sessions a week. Add 5 kg (10 lb) per session while it's easy, then switch to 2.5 kg (5 lb) once the bar starts to slow down.

| Session | kg | lb | Jump next time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 40 x 5, 5, 5 | 90 x 5, 5, 5 | +5 kg / +10 lb |
| 2 | 45 x 5, 5, 5 | 100 x 5, 5, 5 | +5 kg / +10 lb |
| 3 | 50 x 5, 5, 5 | 110 x 5, 5, 5 | +5 kg / +10 lb |
| 4 | 55 x 5, 5, 5 | 120 x 5, 5, 5 | +5 kg / +10 lb |
| 5 | 60 x 5, 5, 5 | 130 x 5, 5, 5 | +2.5 kg / +5 lb |
| 6 | 62.5 x 5, 5, 5 | 135 x 5, 5, 5 | +2.5 kg / +5 lb |
| 7 | 65 x 5, 5, 5 | 140 x 5, 5, 5 | +2.5 kg / +5 lb |
| 8 | 67.5 x 5, 5, 5 | 145 x 5, 5, 5 | +2.5 kg / +5 lb |
| 9 | 70 x 5, 5, 5 | 150 x 5, 5, 5 | +2.5 kg / +5 lb |
| 10 | 72.5 x 5, 5, 3 | 155 x 5, 5, 3 | Repeat |

Thirty kilograms (60 lb) in three weeks looks great, partly because session 1 was deliberately light. Starting light buys weeks of clean practice before the weight gets serious.

### Example 5: intermediate squat, add sets then add load

When weekly load jumps stall, use sets as the in-between step. Keep the weight, add a set each week up to five, then add 2.5 kg (5 lb) and drop back to three sets.

| Week | kg | Volume (kg) | lb | Volume (lb) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 x 3 x 6 | 1,800 | 225 x 3 x 6 | 4,050 |
| 2 | 100 x 4 x 6 | 2,400 | 225 x 4 x 6 | 5,400 |
| 3 | 100 x 5 x 6 | 3,000 | 225 x 5 x 6 | 6,750 |
| 4 | 102.5 x 3 x 6 | 1,845 | 230 x 3 x 6 | 4,140 |
| 5 | 102.5 x 4 x 6 | 2,460 | 230 x 4 x 6 | 5,520 |
| 6 | 102.5 x 5 x 6 | 3,075 | 230 x 5 x 6 | 6,900 |

Volume here is sets x reps x weight, so week 1 is 3 x 6 x 100 = 1,800 kg. There's some research behind adding sets over time. In a 12-week study of trained men, [groups that added sets every two weeks gained more squat strength](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37796222/) than a group that kept sets constant, although the muscle-size results were less certain.

Sets can't climb forever. Five hard sets of squats twice a week is already 10 sets for your quads before any leg press. Our guide to [how many sets per muscle group per week](/blog/how-many-sets-per-muscle-group-per-week) covers where the sensible ceiling sits.

## Progressive overload with dumbbells: reps first, then load

Dumbbells are where weight-only progression falls apart. Most gyms step 2 kg or 2.5 kg per hand, or 5 lb per hand, and on light dumbbells that's a big percentage.

| Jump (per hand) | Increase | Jump (per hand) | Increase |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 kg to 10 kg | 25% | 15 lb to 20 lb | 33% |
| 12 kg to 14 kg | 17% | 25 lb to 30 lb | 20% |
| 20 kg to 22 kg | 10% | 50 lb to 55 lb | 10% |
| 32 kg to 34 kg | 6% | 70 lb to 75 lb | 7% |

Compare that with adding 2.5 kg to a 60 kg bench, which is about 4 percent. So with dumbbells you build reps first, often well past where you'd stop on a barbell, and only then pick up the next pair.

*Photo: [Ivan S](https://www.pexels.com/photo/silver-dumbbells-on-black-steel-rack-4164766/) on Pexels*

### Example 6: lateral raise on a wide rep range

Lateral raises, 3 sets of 12 to 20 per arm. Move up only when all three sets hit 20.

| Session | kg (per hand) | lb (per hand) | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8 x 14, 13, 12 | 15 x 14, 13, 12 | Same weight |
| 2 | 8 x 16, 14, 13 | 15 x 16, 14, 13 | Same weight |
| 3 | 8 x 18, 16, 14 | 15 x 18, 16, 14 | Same weight |
| 4 | 8 x 20, 18, 16 | 15 x 20, 18, 16 | Same weight |
| 5 | 8 x 20, 20, 18 | 15 x 20, 20, 18 | Same weight |
| 6 | 8 x 20, 20, 20 | 15 x 20, 20, 20 | Move up |
| 7 | 10 x 14, 12, 12 | 20 x 13, 12, 11 | Build reps again |

The wide range is what makes session 7 work. A narrow 8 to 12 range would have you land at 10 kg for 7 reps, below the bottom of the range, and stuck.

### Example 7: dumbbell shoulder press with a total-rep target

Three sets of 8 to 12. Instead of needing 12 on every set, move up once your total across all three sets reaches 36. One weak final set then doesn't hold you back for a week.

| Session | kg (per hand) | lb (per hand) | Total reps | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 16 x 10, 9, 8 | 35 x 10, 9, 8 | 27 | Same weight |
| 2 | 16 x 11, 10, 9 | 35 x 11, 10, 9 | 30 | Same weight |
| 3 | 16 x 12, 11, 10 | 35 x 12, 11, 10 | 33 | Same weight |
| 4 | 16 x 12, 12, 11 | 35 x 12, 12, 11 | 35 | Same weight |
| 5 | 16 x 12, 12, 12 | 35 x 12, 12, 12 | 36 | Move up |
| 6 | 18 x 9, 8, 8 | 40 x 9, 8, 7 | 25 / 24 | Build reps |

Two habits make dumbbell examples like these trustworthy. Log the same way every time, per hand or total. Our post on [whether to log dumbbells per hand or total](/blog/log-dumbbells-per-hand-or-total) explains why per hand is usually easier. And if your gym has 1 kg steps or small add-on plates, use them on the light dumbbells.

## Progressive overload push-ups: reps, tempo, deficit, then weight

Push-ups follow the same pattern with different levers. Once you can do 15 or more clean reps, adding more reps turns into an endurance contest. So you make each rep harder instead.

Harder push-ups can build muscle and strength much like a bench press. In one eight-week study, [push-ups loaded to match a 40 percent bench press](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29541130/) built chest and triceps size and bench strength about as well as benching at that load. Another four-week study found [progressively harder push-up variations](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29466268/) raised bench press 1RM in both the push-up and bench groups.

![Man doing push-ups on a gym floor](/blog/progressive-overload-examples/push-ups.webp)
*Photo: [Ivan S](https://www.pexels.com/photo/man-doing-push-ups-4162491/) on Pexels*

### Example 8: a 12-week push-up progression

Three sessions a week, three sets each. Change one lever at a time.

| Weeks | Variation | Target per set | Added weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-3 | Standard push-up | 8, then 10, then 12 reps | None |
| 4 | Standard push-up | 15 reps | None |
| 5-6 | 3-second lowering | 10, then 12 reps | None |
| 7-8 | Deficit (hands on plates or handles, 5-8 cm / 2-3 in up) | 10, then 12 reps | None |
| 9-10 | Feet on a bench | 10, then 12 reps | None |
| 11 | Weighted, feet on floor | 10 reps | 5 kg / 10 lb |
| 12 | Weighted, feet on floor | 10 reps | 10 kg / 20 lb |

If you can't do 8 full push-ups yet, start with your hands on a bench and lower the bench over the weeks. For added weight, a vest is easiest. A plate on the upper back works if someone places it for you, and a loop band over your back adds resistance at the top.

Log each variation as its own exercise, so deficit push-ups don't hide inside your normal push-up history. For weighted push-ups, log the added weight only (+5 kg or +10 lb). In [GymLedger](/), a custom exercise such as "Push-up, deficit" keeps each variation on its own strength chart.

## How often should you progressive overload?

Try to beat the log a little every session, but don't expect to add weight every session. How often load goes up depends mostly on how long you've been training.

| Stage | Bench press | Squat | Dumbbells and isolation | Push-ups |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Beginner** (first few months) | +2.5 kg / 5 lb most sessions | +2.5 to 5 kg / 5 to 10 lb most sessions | Reps each session, load when the range is maxed | 1 to 2 more reps per set each week |
| **Intermediate** | +1 to 2.5 kg / 2.5 to 5 lb every 1 to 4 weeks, reps in between | +2.5 kg / 5 lb every 1 to 3 weeks | New dumbbell every few weeks | Harder variation every 2 weeks |
| **Advanced** | Small PRs at the end of a 4 to 12 week block | Same | Mostly reps and sets | Weighted, small jumps |

These are rough patterns, not promises. On a progressive overload bench press plan, many lifters move from the beginner row to the intermediate row within the first year. The American College of Sports Medicine's [2009 position stand on progression models](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/) gives a handy trigger: when you can do one or two reps more than your target with a given load, increase it by about 2 to 10 percent. On a 60 kg (135 lb) bench, that's 1.2 to 6 kg (about 3 to 13 lb), so for most people the small end of that range is the right call.

In short, progress as often as your log shows you've earned it, whether that's one rep, one set or 1 kg.

## When an example stalls: what to do

Every example above has a week where the numbers don't move. That's normal. What matters is how many weeks in a row it happens, and on how many lifts.

| What the log shows | Likely cause | What to do (kg / lb) |
|---|---|---|
| One session short of target | A bad day | Repeat the weight |
| Same miss twice in a row | The jump is too big | Smaller jumps (1 kg / 2.5 lb) or switch to adding reps |
| One lift flat for three weeks | That lift needs a change | Drop about 10% and rebuild: 80 kg to 72.5 kg (175 lb to 160 lb), then climb in small steps |
| Several lifts falling at once | Built-up fatigue | Take a [deload week](/blog/deload-week) |
| Reps rising but form slipping | Counting bad reps | Hold the weight and clean up the reps |
| Sets feel near-max every week at the same weight | Too heavy for the plan | Rate effort with the [RPE chart](/blog/rpe-chart) and lower the load until sets land around 2 to 3 reps left |

Before you change the program, check sleep, food and rest between sets. Short rest alone can cost you reps on heavy sets. If you're eating in a deficit, holding your bench steady is a fine result.

The "drop 10% and rebuild" fix deserves a worked example. A lifter stuck at 80 kg x 5, 5, 4 (175 lb) for three weeks drops to 72.5 kg (160 lb) for 3 x 5. They add 1 kg (2.5 lb) per week, which gets them back to 80 kg (175 lb) in six to eight weeks, and they usually arrive with all three sets complete and room to keep going. It feels like going backwards for a month, but the old weight comes back with clean reps instead of grinders.

A per-exercise chart makes stalls obvious early. GymLedger draws a strength chart for every exercise, so three flat weeks on bench show up as a flat line before they turn into three flat months.

## Putting it into practice

Pick the example closest to where you are and run it for the next four weeks:

1. **New to lifting:** Examples 1 and 4. Add the smallest plate every session you complete all reps.
2. **Stalling on bench or squat:** Example 2 (microplates) or Example 5 (add sets, then load).
3. **Training with dumbbells:** Examples 6 and 7. Use wide rep ranges and build reps well before moving up.
4. **Training at home:** Example 8. Change one push-up lever every two weeks.
5. **Stuck for three weeks:** Use the stall table, starting with the smallest fix.

Set your rep ranges in a workout template, so the target is in front of you for every set. In GymLedger you can build templates with each exercise's sets and rep range, log in kg or lbs, and watch each lift on its own strength chart. A notebook does the same job if you actually read it.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is an example of progressive overload?

A simple example is benching 60 kg (135 lb) for 3 sets of 5 this week and 62.5 kg (140 lb) for 3 sets of 5 next week. Doing 9 reps where you did 8 last time, adding a fourth set, or switching from standard to deficit push-ups also count. The common thread is doing slightly more work than your last matching session, in a way you can measure and repeat.

### How much weight should I add to my bench press each week?

In a beginner progressive overload bench press plan, you can usually add 2.5 kg (5 lb) per session for the first weeks. After that, most lifters do better adding 1 to 2.5 kg (2.5 to 5 lb) every one to four weeks, with reps or sets as the in-between steps. If you miss reps twice in a row at the same weight, use smaller plates or add reps before adding weight again.

### How often should you progressive overload?

Aim to beat your previous session by a small step every time you train a lift, but add weight only when you have earned it by completing all target reps. Beginners can often add load most sessions. Intermediates usually add load every one to four weeks and progress reps in between. Advanced lifters measure progress across whole training blocks rather than single sessions.

### Can you progressive overload with push-ups?

Yes. Add reps until you reach about 15 per set, then make each rep harder: slow the lowering to three seconds, push up from handles or plates for a deeper range, raise your feet on a bench, and finally add weight with a vest or a plate. Change one variable at a time and log each variation as its own exercise so your history stays comparable.

### How do you progressive overload with dumbbells when the jump is too big?

Build reps first. Use a wide rep range such as 12 to 20 on small exercises, and move to the next dumbbell only when every set reaches the top of that range. A total-rep target across all sets also helps. If your gym has 1 kg steps or add-on plates, use them on light dumbbells where a 2 kg jump can be 20 percent or more.

### Is it okay to repeat the same weight for several weeks?

Yes, as long as something else is improving, such as reps, sets or cleaner technique. Repeating a weight for two or three sessions while reps climb is normal. If the weight and the reps are both flat for three weeks, check sleep, food and rest first, then use a smaller jump, drop about 10 percent and rebuild, or take a deload week.
