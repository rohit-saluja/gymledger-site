How much protein to build muscle? For most people who lift, **about 1.6 g per kg of body weight per day (0.73 g per lb)** gets nearly all the benefit, and **2.2 g per kg (1 g per lb)** is a sensible upper end if you want a margin. Spread it over three to five meals of roughly 0.4 g per kg each. Hitting the daily total most days matters far more than timing, shakes or any one meal.

The rest of this guide covers the research behind those two numbers, a grams-per-day table for body weights from 50 to 110 kg, how the target moves when you cut or bulk, what "too much" means for a healthy lifter, and two sample days built from dal, paneer, curd, eggs, chicken and soya.

## How much protein to build muscle: the short answer

| Who | Grams per kg per day | Grams per lb per day |
|---|---|---|
| Adult who doesn't train (RDA, the minimum to avoid deficiency) | 0.8 | 0.36 |
| Lifting to build muscle (the evidence-based target) | 1.6 | 0.73 |
| Upper end of the useful range for muscle gain | 2.2 | 1.0 |
| Lean, trained lifter in a calorie deficit | 2.3-3.1 per kg of fat-free mass | 1.0-1.4 per lb of fat-free mass |

If you remember one line, make it this: **1.6 g/kg is the target, and 2.2 g/kg is the most that's likely to help.** Anywhere between the two, eaten consistently, covers protein for muscle gain for almost everyone who trains.

The RDA of 0.8 g/kg is set to prevent deficiency in people who don't train. It was never meant as a target for someone trying to add muscle, and the meta-analysis below concluded it falls short for that job.

## Where 1.6 and 2.2 g per kg come from

Most evidence-based answers to how much protein to build muscle trace back to two sources: a large meta-analysis and a sports nutrition position stand.

### The Morton 2018 meta-analysis

The most-cited figure comes from a [2018 meta-analysis by Morton and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/) in the British Journal of Sports Medicine. It pooled 49 randomised trials with 1,863 participants, all doing at least six weeks of resistance training, some with extra protein and some without.

Three findings matter for you:

1. **Muscle gain levelled off at about 1.62 g/kg/day.** On average, eating more than that added no further fat-free mass.
2. **The 95% confidence interval for that plateau ran from 1.03 to 2.20 g/kg/day.** Some people may sit near the top of that range, so the authors suggested about 2.2 g/kg for anyone trying to get every last bit of muscle from their training.
3. **Training mattered far more than protein.** Extra protein added about 0.30 kg of fat-free mass and 2.49 kg to one-rep max strength across the studies. The training drove most of the gains.

So 1.6 is the best single estimate, and 2.2 covers the people at the high end. [Examine's protein guide](https://examine.com/guides/protein-intake/) lands on the same 1.6-2.2 g/kg range for muscle gain and notes that the extra gain above 1.6 is small.

One limit is worth knowing. Morton's analysis only included people eating at or above maintenance calories. Protein needs in a deficit are higher, as you'll see further down.

### The ISSN position stand

The [International Society of Sports Nutrition's 2017 position stand on protein](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/) puts the range at **1.4-2.0 g/kg/day** for most exercising people who want to build or keep muscle. It adds that resistance-trained people may need **2.3-3.1 g/kg/day** to hold on to lean mass while dieting.

The two sources agree well. Morton's plateau at 1.6 sits in the middle of the ISSN range, and both say the number climbs when calories drop.

### Working it out yourself

In kg, multiply your body weight by 1.6. A 72 kg lifter needs 72 × 1.6 = **115 g a day**.

In lb, multiply by 0.73. A 180 lb lifter needs 180 × 0.73 = **131 g a day**. At the top of the range, 180 × 1.0 = 180 g.

This is also the figure GymLedger uses: its Diet tab sets your daily protein target from your body weight at 1.6 g per kg.

### The "1 gram per pound" rule

The old gym rule of 1 g per lb works out to 2.2 g/kg, the top of the useful range. That's a generous target. If you hit it easily and like the food, carry on. If it means forcing down a third shake every day, drop to 0.7-0.8 g per lb and you'll give up very little, if anything.

### Current weight or goal weight?

Use your current weight if you're reasonably lean. If you carry a lot of body fat, 1.6 g per kg of total weight can give a number that's hard to eat and probably more than you need. Examine suggests that adults with overweight or obesity who are losing fat can aim for 1.2-1.6 g/kg.

A simple fix many lifters use is to calculate from goal weight instead. A 110 kg (242 lb) person aiming for 90 kg (198 lb) would use 90 × 1.6 = **144 g** instead of 176 g. That's still plenty to support training, and it's a target they can hit on real food.

## How much protein per meal

The daily total matters most. How you split it still helps a little, and a sensible split makes the total easier to reach.

The ISSN suggests about **0.25 g per kg per meal, or 20-40 g**, spread every three to four hours. A [2019 review of off-season bodybuilding nutrition by Iraki and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31247944/) goes a bit higher: **0.40-0.55 g per kg per meal** over three to six meals, including one within one to two hours before and after training.

A practical middle ground is **about 0.4 g per kg per meal, four times a day**, which lands right on 1.6 g/kg.

| Body weight | Per meal at 0.4 g/kg | Four meals give |
|---|---|---|
| 60 kg (132 lb) | 24 g | 96 g |
| 70 kg (154 lb) | 28 g | 112 g |
| 80 kg (176 lb) | 32 g | 128 g |
| 90 kg (198 lb) | 36 g | 144 g |
| 100 kg (220 lb) | 40 g | 160 g |

Three meals works too. A 75 kg (165 lb) lifter aiming for 120 g can eat 40 g at breakfast, lunch and dinner. Five smaller feeds of 24 g get there as well. Pick the pattern you'll actually keep.

### The "30 g per meal" myth

You'll hear that your body can only use 30 g of protein at a time and the rest is wasted. Examine's review of the research disagrees. The body uses all the protein you eat sooner or later, and there's no practical ceiling on one meal beyond what your gut can handle. One 2023 trial it describes found that 100 g of milk protein after training kept muscle protein synthesis about 40% higher than 25 g did, from four to twelve hours afterwards.

So a 60 g dinner isn't half thrown away. The real reason to spread protein out is convenience. Four moderate meals are easier to eat than one enormous one, and each gives your muscles a fresh dose.

## Protein timing: total matters most

The "anabolic window", the idea that you need a shake within 30 minutes of your last set, has been oversold. The ISSN notes that the muscle-building effect of a training session lasts at least 24 hours and that eating protein before or after training is largely a matter of what suits you. Morton's team concluded that the details of protein supplementation (timing, post-workout dose, protein source) play a minor role, if any, in muscle and strength gains over weeks of training. A contest-prep review by [Helms and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864135/) reached a similar view on meal timing and frequency.

What that means day to day:

- **Hit your daily number first.** Five days at 130 g beat three days of perfectly timed shakes followed by two days at 60 g.
- **Eat a protein meal within a few hours either side of training.** If you lift at 6 pm and had lunch at 1 pm, dinner after the gym covers it. Your gains will survive the drive home.
- **Train fasted? Eat soon after.** If you lift first thing without breakfast, your last meal was the night before, so make the first meal after training a protein-rich one.
- **Use the evening if you're short.** If dinner is early and you're behind on the day's number, a protein snack before bed is an easy place to make up the gap.

## Protein when cutting vs bulking

Your protein target shifts with your calories. The less you eat overall, the more protein you need to keep the muscle you already have.

### Bulking: a calorie surplus

In a surplus, 1.6 g/kg is plenty. The extra calories already make muscle gain easier, and the Iraki review puts the off-season range at 1.6-2.2 g/kg. Pushing protein to 3 g/kg on a bulk mostly takes the place of carbohydrate that would have fuelled your training. Spend the extra calories on rice, roti, oats and potatoes, not a fourth scoop of whey.

### Cutting: a calorie deficit

Here the number goes up. A [systematic review by Helms and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24092765/) on lean, resistance-trained athletes in a deficit put protein needs at **2.3-3.1 g per kg of fat-free mass**, scaled up the leaner you are and the bigger the deficit. The ISSN's 2.3-3.1 g/kg for dieting lifters points the same way.

Fat-free mass is your body weight minus your fat. For an 80 kg (176 lb) lifter at about 15% body fat:

1. Fat-free mass: 80 × 0.85 = 68 kg (150 lb)
2. Low end: 68 × 2.3 = **156 g a day**
3. High end: 68 × 3.1 = **211 g a day**

That works out to roughly 2.0-2.6 g per kg of total body weight. The top of that range is for lean lifters on a hard diet, such as the final weeks before a physique show. Most people on a moderate cut sit comfortably nearer the bottom.

| Phase | Protein target | 75 kg (165 lb) lifter |
|---|---|---|
| Lean bulk | 1.6-2.2 g/kg | 120-165 g |
| Maintenance or recomposition | 1.6-2.2 g/kg | 120-165 g |
| Cut, lean or athletic (Examine's range) | 1.6-2.4 g/kg | 120-180 g |
| Cut, carrying a lot of fat (Examine's range) | 1.2-1.6 g/kg | 90-120 g |
| Lean lifter, hard cut (Helms) | 2.3-3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass | about 150-200 g at 13% body fat |

For the full comparison of the two phases, including calorie targets and how long each should last, read [bulking vs cutting](/blog/bulking-vs-cutting). If you're new to lifting or coming back after a break, you may be able to lose fat and gain muscle at the same time, and protein sits at the centre of that; see [body recomposition](/blog/body-recomposition). Whichever phase you're in, judge it by your [weekly average body weight](/blog/how-often-to-weigh-yourself), not one morning on the scale.

## Protein: how much is too much for a healthy lifter

For healthy people, the evidence so far says high intakes aren't harmful over the periods studied. The ISSN reviewed this directly and reported that published reviews consistently show no sign of kidney or liver harm from higher protein in athletes and active people. It also summarises trials by Antonio and colleagues in which resistance-trained people ate roughly 3.4-4.4 g/kg/day for eight weeks with no harmful changes in blood markers of kidney or liver function. Examine notes that the few studies of 2.8 g/kg and above found them safe for healthy people, and that longer trials are still needed.

The caveats are real, though:

- **Most of these studies are short**, from eight weeks to about a year, and they used healthy, trained adults.
- **Kidney disease is a different situation.** The ISSN itself notes that people with kidney failure benefit from protein-restricted diets. If you have kidney disease, diabetes, are pregnant, or have any other medical condition, check with your doctor before raising your protein.
- **More protein doesn't mean more muscle.** Morton found no extra gain above about 1.6 g/kg on average. Past 2.2 g/kg you're probably paying for protein your muscles won't use for growth.

For a healthy lifter, then, the limits of a very high intake are practical ones. Protein foods are usually the priciest part of the shopping list. Protein that pushes out rice, fruit, vegetables and pulses leaves less fuel for training and less fibre for digestion. And a target that turns every meal into a chore is one you'll probably drop.

The sweet spot for most people is 1.6-2.2 g/kg. Eat above 3 g/kg day after day and you're well past any proven benefit for muscle.

A few things stand out:

- **Count dal by its dry weight.** How much protein is in your bowl depends on how much dry dal went into the pot per person. Fifty grams of dry masoor or moong gives about 12 g; a thin dal stretched across a family gives far less.
- **Curd and Greek yogurt are not the same.** A bowl of regular curd has about a third of the protein of the same weight of Greek yogurt. Hung curd, strained the same way, sits closer to Greek yogurt.
- **Roti and rice add up.** Grains aren't protein-dense, but four rotis still bring around 18 g.
- **Soya chunks punch hard.** At about 51 g of protein per 100 g dry, they're one of the densest vegetarian options going.

For vegetarians and vegans, the answer to how much protein to build muscle stays the same. Examine suggests aiming for at least 1.6 g/kg and eating a variety of plant sources. Mixing pulses, grains, soy and dairy across the day covers this without any special planning.

### If you keep falling short

- **Add one protein food to the meal that's lightest.** For most people that's breakfast. Two eggs or a bowl of Greek yogurt adds 12-20 g.
- **Make dal thicker or add a second pulse.** A cup of chana or rajma next to the dal adds about 15 g.
- **Keep an easy snack around.** Boiled eggs, roasted chana, peanuts, a glass of milk.
- **Use whey for convenience.** The ISSN describes supplements as a practical way to get enough good-quality protein. Think of a scoop of whey as fast food for protein, nothing more.

## Putting it into practice

1. **Work out your target.** Body weight in kg × 1.6, or lb × 0.73. If you carry a lot of fat, use goal weight.
2. **Split it into meals.** About 0.4 g per kg per meal, three to five times a day.
3. **Build every meal around one protein food.** Eggs, chicken, paneer, tofu, soya, Greek yogurt or a proper serving of dal.
4. **Log it for two weeks.** Most people find they're well under on one or two meals and fine on the rest.
5. **Adjust for your phase.** Stay at 1.6-2.2 g/kg on a bulk or at maintenance; move towards 2.2 or higher on a cut, especially if you're lean.
6. **Keep training hard and progressively.** Protein for muscle gain only works if you give the muscle a reason to grow.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much protein do I need a day to build muscle?

Most people who lift build muscle well on about 1.6 g of protein per kg of body weight per day, which is 0.73 g per lb. A 70 kg (154 lb) lifter would aim for about 112 g. Going up to 2.2 g per kg (1 g per lb) may help a few people slightly, but research shows little extra gain above 1.6 for most. Spread it across three to five meals.

### Is 1 gram of protein per pound too much?

One gram per pound equals 2.2 g per kg, the top of the range that research links to extra muscle gain. For a healthy person it isn't harmful, but it's more than most people need. Eating 0.7-0.8 g per pound gives nearly the same results with less food and expense. If you have kidney disease or another medical condition, ask your doctor what's right for you.

### How much protein is too much in a day?

For healthy adults, trials in resistance-trained people eating roughly 3.4-4.4 g per kg for eight weeks found no harmful changes in kidney or liver markers. Above about 2.2 g per kg, though, extra protein doesn't build more muscle; it just replaces other food. If you have kidney disease or diabetes, or are pregnant, check with your doctor, because your limits may be different.

### Can you build muscle on a vegetarian Indian diet?

Yes. Dal, paneer, curd, milk, soya chunks, tofu, chana and rajma can get most lifters to 1.6 g per kg without supplements. The catch is portion size: count dal by dry weight, choose Greek yogurt or hung curd over thin curd when you can, and put one protein-rich food in every meal. A 70 kg vegetarian can reach about 116 g a day this way.

### Should I calculate protein from my current weight or goal weight?

Use your current weight if you're reasonably lean. If you carry a lot of body fat, multiplying total weight by 1.6 gives a high number that's hard to eat and probably more than you need. Using goal weight instead is a common fix: a 110 kg person aiming for 90 kg would eat about 144 g a day instead of 176 g.

### Do I need a protein shake right after a workout?

No. The muscle-building effect of a session lasts at least 24 hours, and research finds that protein timing plays a minor role compared with your daily total. Eat a protein-rich meal within a few hours before or after training and hit your number for the day. A shake is simply a quick way to do that.
