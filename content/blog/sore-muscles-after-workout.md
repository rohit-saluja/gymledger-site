Sore muscles after workout sessions are usually delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS). It usually shows up 12 to 48 hours after training, peaks around 48 to 72 hours, and is mostly gone within five days. It tells you that you did something new or put the muscle through a lot of lowering work. It says very little about how good the workout was. Mild soreness is fine to train through; if you can't move a muscle through its full range, give it another day and train something else.

Soreness is only one part of recovery, and often the least useful one for deciding when to train. For the bigger picture, including recovery times by muscle group, see our guide to [how long it takes for muscles to recover](/blog/how-long-does-it-take-for-muscles-to-recover).

## Sore muscles after workout sessions: what DOMS is

DOMS is the stiff, tender, "don't make me walk down stairs" feeling that shows up the day after a hard or unfamiliar session. Current reviews describe it as small-scale damage inside the muscle fibres, caused mainly by lowering (eccentric) work and unfamiliar exercise, followed by a local inflammatory response. That chain of events takes time, which is why the soreness is delayed, typically peaking 48 to 72 hours after the session ([Hotfiel et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30537791/)).

Lactic acid used to get the blame. It's one of several old theories, and reviews now point to muscle damage and inflammation instead ([Cheung et al., 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12617692/)). So there's nothing to "flush out".

It helps to separate three feelings that people lump together:

| Feeling | When it happens | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| The burn during a set | During the set, gone within minutes | Fatigue in the working muscle; harmless |
| DOMS | Starts 12 to 48 hours later, peaks around 2 to 3 days | Normal soreness after new or hard work |
| Sharp pain | During a rep, often in one spot or near a joint | Possibly an injury; stop that exercise |

### The DOMS timeline

| Time after the session | What you'll usually notice |
|---|---|
| 0 to 12 hours | Not much. Maybe tired, maybe a pump. |
| 12 to 48 hours | Stiffness creeps in and builds. Getting out of a chair becomes a project. |
| 48 to 72 hours | Usually the peak. Tender to touch, stiff on the first few reps of anything. |
| 3 to 5 days | Fading. Most people are close to normal by day five. |
| 7 days or more | Not typical DOMS (see the red flags below). |

Cleveland Clinic's guidance matches this: soreness usually lasts a few days, rarely more than five, and soreness lasting a week or more may point to an injury such as a strain ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/delayed-onset-muscle-soreness)).

## Why new exercises and lowering work make you so sore

Two things reliably cause DOMS: **eccentric work** (lowering a weight under control) and **anything your muscles aren't used to**. A review of DOMS research found eccentric actions cause muscle micro-injury more often and more severely than other muscle actions, and that DOMS is most common when athletes return after time off or try a new type of activity (Cheung et al., 2003).

The usual suspects in a gym:

- Romanian deadlifts and stiff-legged deadlifts
- Walking lunges and Bulgarian split squats
- Slow negatives, or any tempo with a 3-second lowering phase
- Deep-stretch exercises like dumbbell flyes and incline curls
- Your first leg day after a month off

### The repeated bout effect: why it gets easier fast

Your body adapts quickly. A single bout of eccentric exercise protects the muscle against damage from later bouts, which is known as the repeated bout effect ([McHugh, 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12641640/)). That's why your first week of Bulgarian split squats is miserable and your fourth barely registers.

A 10-week study saw the same pattern in a normal training program: muscle damage after a session was highest in week one, lower by week three and minimal by week ten ([Damas et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27219125/)).

### Soreness is not a measure of a good workout

The same study also measured muscle protein synthesis. In week one, when damage was highest, muscle protein synthesis was also high, but it wasn't related to muscle growth. Once the damage had faded, in weeks three and ten, protein synthesis tracked growth closely. In plain terms, the early response mostly went into repair.

More soreness, then, doesn't mean more growth. If you've stopped getting sore muscles after workout sessions on your program but your weights and reps keep creeping up in your log, the program is working.

## Working out with sore muscles: train or rest?

Most of the time, train. Working out with sore muscles is usually safe, and light exercise is one of the few things that reliably eases the pain for a while. Cheung's review called exercise the most effective way to relieve DOMS pain, though the relief is temporary. The question is how hard to train the sore muscle.

A practical scale: rate the soreness from 0 to 10 after you've warmed up, not when you first get out of bed. A sports medicine doctor quoted by UVA Health uses a similar rule, calling 1 to 3 out of 10 fine and suggesting you back off if it goes above that, gets worse during activity or changes the way you walk ([UVA Health](https://www.uvahealth.com/healthy-balance/avoiding-post-workout-soreness-rhabdomyolysis)).

| Soreness after warming up | What it feels like | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 3 out of 10 | Stiff for the first few reps, then normal | Train as planned |
| 4 to 6 out of 10 | Tender all day; the warm-up helps but doesn't fix it | Train the muscle lighter (one set fewer per exercise, 3 reps in reserve) or train other muscles today |
| 7 out of 10 or more | Can't use the full range of motion, limping, stairs are a negotiation | Rest that muscle; train other muscles or go for a walk |
| Sharp, one-spot pain, swelling or dark urine | Not normal soreness | Stop that exercise and read the red flags below |

This lines up with the advice in Cheung's review: if you have to train daily, reduce intensity and duration for one to two days after a session that caused heavy DOMS, or train the less affected muscles.

### The warm-up test

Use your warm-up sets to decide. Do two or three ramp-up sets of your first exercise. If the stiffness fades and the bar moves at its normal speed by the last warm-up set, go ahead with your planned weights. If the soreness gets worse or you can't hit full depth, switch to the lighter version or move to a different session.

### Worked example: sore quads on a push/pull/legs week

You did legs on Monday: squat 4 × 6 at **100 kg (220 lb)** and Romanian deadlift 3 × 8 at **80 kg (175 lb)**. You ran RDLs for the first time in months, and on Thursday, legs day again, your hamstrings are a 5 out of 10 after warming up. Your quads feel fine.

- **Squat:** keep it, but do 3 × 6 at **95 kg (210 lb)** instead of 4 × 6 at 100 kg.
- **Romanian deadlift:** 2 × 8 at **60 kg (130 lb)**. Light, smooth reps through a comfortable range.
- **Leg curl:** 2 sets of 12, stopped well short of failure.
- **Everything else:** as planned.

By next Monday, the repeated bout effect has done its work and you're usually back to full sets. If you train a rolling split and simply aren't up for legs, do the next session in your rotation and come back to legs in a day or two.

## How to ease sore muscles: what helps and what's overhyped

There's no switch that turns DOMS off. Some things take the edge off, and a few have decent evidence behind them. A 2018 meta-analysis of 99 studies found that active recovery, massage, compression garments, water immersion and cryotherapy all reduced DOMS by small to large amounts, with massage the most effective for both soreness and perceived fatigue ([Dupuy et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29755363/)).

| Method | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Light movement (walking, easy cycling, light sets of the sore movement) | Helps, though the relief is temporary |
| Massage | The best-supported option in the 2018 meta-analysis |
| Foam rolling | A cheap do-it-yourself massage; if it feels good, use it |
| Compression garments, water immersion | Small to moderate effects on soreness and fatigue; fine if you like them |
| Cold or heat | Mixed or thin evidence; use whichever feels better |
| Static stretching | Little to no effect on soreness in the research |
| Training through severe soreness at full intensity | Usually makes the rest of the week worse |

### Light movement first

A 20 to 30 minute walk, an easy bike ride or a few light sets of the sore movement is the simplest answer to how to ease sore muscles. It costs nothing, fits on a rest day, and both reviews cited here found that moving the sore muscle eases the pain for a while.

### Sleep and protein: the basics still count

Sleep and food won't make DOMS vanish overnight, but they're what let you keep training well while you adapt. Get your protein in every day: around 1.6 g per kg of body weight is a common target, which is about 120 g for a 75 kg (165 lb) lifter. Our guide to [how much protein to build muscle](/blog/how-much-protein-to-build-muscle) has the details. If you track food in GymLedger, the Diet tab lets you type what you ate in plain words, estimates the protein, and the dashboard shows today's total against a target set from your body weight.

### Go easy on painkillers before training

UVA Health lists taking ibuprofen or naproxen while exercising among the risk factors for rhabdomyolysis, a serious form of muscle breakdown. Don't take one so you can train through heavy soreness.

## How to prevent sore muscles after workout sessions

You can't avoid DOMS completely, and you don't need to. You can stop it from wrecking your first two weeks on a new program. Cheung's review recommends introducing eccentric or new exercises gradually over one to two weeks, and that's the core of it.

### Ramp volume in week one

When you start a new program, don't do the full program in week one. Cut the sets and leave more reps in the tank, then build up:

| Week | Sets per exercise | Effort |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | About half the program's sets (2 instead of 4) | 3 to 4 reps short of failure |
| 2 | Three quarters (3 instead of 4) | 2 to 3 reps short of failure |
| 3 | Full program (4 sets) | As the program says |

You lose very little by doing this, since the first week of a new program mostly builds tolerance, and you'll be able to train every planned session instead of skipping the second leg day because you can't sit down.

### Bring new exercises in light

**Worked example: adding Romanian deadlifts.** Say your conventional deadlift is 140 kg (310 lb) for 5. Don't start RDLs at 100 kg (220 lb) for 3 hard sets of 8.

- **Week 1:** 2 × 8 at **50 kg (110 lb)**, slow and controlled
- **Week 2:** 3 × 8 at **60 kg (130 lb)**
- **Week 3:** 3 × 8 at **70 kg (155 lb)**
- **After that:** add 2.5 to 5 kg (5 to 10 lb) when you hit all your reps

For more ways to add weight, reps or sets in small steps, see our [progressive overload examples](/blog/progressive-overload-examples).

### Keep training the movement

The repeated bout effect fades if you leave long gaps. Training a muscle about twice a week keeps it adapted, so the soreness from each session stays small. The lifter who does legs once every ten days gets sore every single time.

### Ease back in after a break

After two weeks or more off, treat your first week back like week one of a new program: fewer sets and lighter loads, then build up over two or three sessions. If you've missed time on push/pull/legs, our guide on what to do when you've [missed a day on push/pull/legs](/blog/missed-a-day-push-pull-legs) has return weights for breaks of different lengths.

A warm-up is worth doing for your working sets anyway, but don't expect it to stop DOMS on its own. The UVA Health doctor notes that the science on preventing soreness is still unsettled.

## When you're always sore

Occasional soreness after a new exercise is normal. If you're sore all the time, week after week, on a program you've been running for a month or more, something else is going on. Usually it's too much volume per session, too many sets taken to failure, or not enough recovery between sessions for the same muscle.

Check your log. If your lifts are also stalling or dropping across several sessions, you probably need a lighter week, not more foam rolling. ## Red flags: when it isn't normal soreness

Normal DOMS is a dull, spread-out ache in the muscle you trained, and it improves each day after the peak. These signs point to something else:

- **Sharp pain** during a rep, especially in one spot or near a joint
- **Obvious swelling**, or a muscle that feels hard and tight beyond normal stiffness
- **Dark, tea-coloured or cola-coloured urine**, or passing very little urine
- **Soreness far out of proportion** to the workout, or soreness that isn't improving after about three days
- **Pain lasting longer than a week**

Dark urine with severe muscle pain can be a sign of rhabdomyolysis, which needs same-day medical attention ([UVA Health](https://www.uvahealth.com/healthy-balance/avoiding-post-workout-soreness-rhabdomyolysis)). For any of these signs, see a doctor.

## Putting it into practice

1. Expect DOMS after new exercises, more lowering work, or a return from a break. It peaks around day two or three and fades by day five.
2. Rate soreness after warming up. At 0 to 3 out of 10, train as planned. At 4 to 6, train the muscle lighter or train something else. At 7 or more, rest that muscle.
3. To ease it, walk, move the sore muscle lightly, and use massage or foam rolling if you like them. Skip the stretching marathon.
4. To prevent it, run week one of any new program at about half the sets, start new exercises light, and train each muscle about twice a week.
5. If you're sore all the time and your lifts are stalling, plan a deload instead of pushing through.
6. Take the red flags seriously. Normal soreness never comes with dark urine.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long do sore muscles last after a workout?

Delayed onset muscle soreness usually starts 12 to 48 hours after a workout, peaks around 48 to 72 hours, and is mostly gone within five days. The first time you do a new exercise, or after a long break, it can sit at the longer end. Soreness that lasts more than a week, or keeps getting worse, isn't typical DOMS.

### Is it OK to work out with sore muscles?

Usually, yes. If the soreness is mild and fades as you warm up, train as planned. If it's moderate, train the sore muscle lighter or train other muscles that day. If you can't move through a full range of motion, rest that muscle. Light exercise often eases soreness for a while, so a complete rest day isn't always the best option.

### Does being sore mean I had a good workout?

No. Soreness mostly tells you the workout was new or heavy on lowering work. As your body adapts, you'll get much less sore from the same program even while you keep getting stronger. Research on early training found the first sessions' muscle-building response went mainly into repair. Judge a workout by your weights and reps over time, not by how much it hurts.

### How can I ease sore muscles quickly?

Light movement works best for quick relief: a walk, an easy bike ride, or a few light sets of the movement that made you sore. Massage has the strongest evidence of the passive options, and foam rolling is a cheap substitute. Compression and cold water help some people a little. None of them makes soreness disappear, so plan your training around it as well.

### Why do I get so sore after not working out for a while?

During a break you lose the protection your muscles built from repeated training, known as the repeated bout effect. Your first sessions back act like brand-new exercise, so soreness comes back in full. Cut your sets roughly in half and use lighter weights for the first week, and the soreness will drop quickly within two or three sessions.
