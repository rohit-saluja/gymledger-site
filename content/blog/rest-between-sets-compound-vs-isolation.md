When it comes to rest between sets, compound vs isolation exercises need different clocks. Heavy compound lifts like squats, deadlifts and bench press usually need **2-3 minutes** (up to 5 for very heavy strength work), while isolation exercises like curls, lateral raises and leg extensions are well served by **60-90 seconds**. The goal is simple: rest long enough that your next set hits the reps you planned, and no longer.

That's the short answer. The rest of this guide explains why the difference exists, what the research actually shows, and how to set rest times you'll stick to when the gym is busy or you only have 45 minutes.

## Why rest periods matter more than most lifters think

Rest is not dead time. It is the part of the workout that decides how much quality work you can do in the next set.

During a hard set, your muscles burn through their short-term energy stores and fatigue builds up. A couple of minutes of rest restores most of that capacity. Cut the rest too short and your next set loses reps or load. Over a whole session, that adds up to less total work, and total work (often called volume load: sets x reps x weight) is one of the main drivers of both strength and muscle gain.

Here's a quick example. Say you squat **100 kg (220 lb) for 8 reps**.

- With 3 minutes of rest, you might hit 8, 8, 7 across three sets: 23 reps, or **2,300 kg (5,060 lb)** of volume load.
- With 60 seconds of rest, you might hit 8, 6, 5: 19 reps, or **1,900 kg (4,180 lb)**.

Same exercise, same weight, same number of sets, and roughly 17% less work. That gap is the whole reason rest periods deserve a plan.

## What the research says about rest between sets

The science on rest intervals has shifted over the last decade. Older guidelines often recommended short rests (30-90 seconds) for muscle growth, on the theory that more metabolic stress meant more hypertrophy. Newer studies have mostly pushed back on that.

### Longer rest helped trained lifters gain more

A 2016 study by Schoenfeld and colleagues compared **1 minute vs 3 minutes** of rest in resistance-trained men over 8 weeks ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/)). The 3-minute group gained more strength in both the squat and bench press, and showed greater muscle thickness in the front of the thigh. Muscular endurance did not differ between the groups.

The likely explanation is the volume effect above. Longer rest let the lifters keep more reps and load on every set.

### Short and long rest can both build muscle

A 2017 systematic review by Grgic and colleagues looked at short (60 seconds or less) versus long (more than 60 seconds) rest intervals ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28641044/)). The authors concluded both can support hypertrophy, with a possible edge for longer rest in trained people, while noting more research was needed.

### The most recent meta-analysis: over 60 seconds, small benefit

The most thorough look so far is a 2024 Bayesian meta-analysis by Singer and colleagues, titled "Give it a rest" ([Frontiers in Sports and Active Living](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/full)). Pooling nine studies, they found a **small hypertrophy benefit for resting longer than 60 seconds**, and no appreciable extra benefit beyond about **90 seconds**.

Two caveats matter. Most participants were young, untrained people in short studies, and the authors did not give separate recommendations for compound and isolation exercises. They did suggest exercise complexity might affect fatigue, which is exactly where practical coaching fills the gap.

### What this means for you

Putting it together:

1. **Very short rest (under 60 seconds) is rarely ideal** for growth or strength, because it costs you reps.
2. **About 60-90 seconds is a reasonable floor** for most hypertrophy work.
3. **Heavy, demanding lifts benefit from more**, because they take longer to recover from, both locally and in terms of whole-body fatigue.

## Rest between sets for compound vs isolation exercises

Here is why the rest between sets for compound vs isolation lifts should differ, even within the same workout and the same goal.

**Compound exercises** (squat, deadlift, bench press, overhead press, rows, pull-ups, lunges) move several joints and a lot of muscle at once. They tax your heart rate, breathing, grip, core bracing and focus. After a hard set of 8 squats, your quads may be ready before your lungs and nervous system are.

**Isolation exercises** (biceps curls, triceps pushdowns, lateral raises, leg extensions, leg curls, calf raises) train one joint and a smaller amount of muscle. The fatigue is local. You're out of breath far less, and the target muscle recovers faster in absolute terms because less total work was done.

So the practical answer to "rest time for squats vs curls" is: squats get roughly twice as long, sometimes three times.

### The "ready" test

A useful self-check before every set: **could you match your last set's reps with good form right now?** If your breathing has settled, your grip feels solid and your head is clear, go. If you'd be grinding out reps just to stay on the clock, take another 30 seconds.

A timer keeps this honest. Without one, most people either rush (and lose reps) or drift into scrolling their phone for six minutes.

## Rest time table by exercise type and goal

Use this as a starting point. Adjust based on how you feel and whether you're hitting your target reps.

| Exercise type | Examples | Strength (1-5 reps) | Hypertrophy (6-12 reps) | Endurance / pump (15+ reps) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Heavy lower-body compound | Squat, deadlift, Romanian deadlift, leg press | 3-5 min | 2-3 min | 90 s-2 min |
| Heavy upper-body compound | Bench press, overhead press, weighted pull-up, barbell row | 3-4 min | 2-3 min | 60-90 s |
| Lighter compound / machine | Dumbbell press, lat pulldown, cable row, lunges | 2-3 min | 90 s-2 min | 60-90 s |
| Large-muscle isolation | Leg extension, leg curl, chest fly | 2 min | 60-120 s | 45-60 s |
| Small-muscle isolation | Curls, pushdowns, lateral raises, calf raises | 90 s-2 min | 60-90 s | 30-60 s |

A few notes on the table:

- **Rest periods for strength** are the longest because heavy sets near your max depend on full recovery. Rushing a top set of 3 at 140 kg (310 lb) is how you miss reps or lose technique.
- **How long to rest between sets for hypertrophy** depends more on the exercise than the goal. A set of 10 on the leg press takes more out of you than a set of 10 curls.
- The endurance column is for high-rep finishers and conditioning-style work. If your goal is building muscle, don't default to it just because shorter feels "harder".

### Worked example: a push day

Here's how rest might look across one session aimed at muscle growth:

1. **Bench press**, 4 sets of 6-8 at 80 kg (175 lb): rest 2.5-3 minutes.
2. **Seated dumbbell press**, 3 sets of 8-10 with 24 kg (55 lb) dumbbells: rest 2 minutes.
3. **Cable chest fly**, 3 sets of 12-15: rest 75 seconds.
4. **Lateral raise**, 3 sets of 12-15 with 8 kg (17.5 lb) dumbbells: rest 60 seconds.
5. **Triceps pushdown**, 3 sets of 10-12: rest 60-75 seconds.

Notice the pattern: rest shrinks as the exercises get smaller and less demanding. Your first lift gets your best effort and the most recovery.

## How rest fits into progressive overload

Rest time is a hidden variable in your training log. If you rested 90 seconds last week and 4 minutes this week, that extra rep you got might just be extra rest, not extra strength.

To make real comparisons week to week, **keep rest roughly consistent for each exercise**. Then when your numbers move, you know it's progress. This matters especially if you use a rep-range method like [double progression](/blog/double-progression), where you add reps until you hit the top of a range before adding weight.

## Supersets and antagonist pairing

Supersets are a popular way to save time. Instead of resting fully between sets of one exercise, you alternate between two exercises with little or no rest between them, then rest after the pair.

### Antagonist pairs

The most useful version for most lifters is the **antagonist superset**: pairing exercises for opposing muscle groups. The muscle you just trained rests while the other one works.

Common pairs:

- Bench press + barbell row (chest / back)
- Overhead press + pull-up or lat pulldown (shoulders / lats)
- Biceps curl + triceps pushdown
- Leg extension + leg curl (quads / hamstrings)

A typical format: do set 1 of exercise A, rest about 60-90 seconds, do set 1 of exercise B, rest 60-90 seconds, and repeat. Each muscle still gets roughly 2-3 minutes between its own sets, but you finish the block in about half the time.

### When supersets work less well

Supersets get harder to manage with heavy lower-body compounds. Pairing heavy squats with heavy deadlifts, for example, means both lifts drain the same whole-body fatigue reserve, and your performance on both will drop. Keep your heaviest compound lifts as straight sets with full rest, and use supersets for accessory and isolation work.

Supersets in a crowded gym can also mean holding two pieces of equipment at once. Be considerate, or pair a station with something you can do with a single pair of dumbbells.

## Time-limited sessions: how to rest less without losing progress

Sometimes you only have 40 minutes. Here's how to trim rest without wrecking your training quality.

1. **Protect rest on your first big lift.** Give your main compound movement full rest. It's where your strength progress lives.
2. **Superset your accessories.** Pair antagonist isolation exercises, as above, to cut accessory time roughly in half.
3. **Drop a set rather than rushing all of them.** Three good sets beat four rushed ones. If you're short on time, remove the last set of an accessory instead of cutting rest on everything.
4. **Pick exercises with fast setup.** Machines and cables let you start the next set quickly, whereas loading a barbell for a new lift eats minutes. If you do train with a barbell, our guide on [how to load a barbell in kg](/blog/how-to-load-a-barbell-in-kg) can speed things up.
5. **Shorten isolation rest first.** Moving curls from 90 to 60 seconds costs far less than moving squats from 3 minutes to 90 seconds.

### Example: 45-minute upper-body session

| Block | Exercise | Sets x reps | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Bench press, 70 kg (155 lb) | 3 x 6-8 | 2.5 min |
| B1 | Lat pulldown | 3 x 8-10 | 75 s |
| B2 | Seated dumbbell press, 20 kg (45 lb) dumbbells | 3 x 8-10 | 75 s |
| C1 | Dumbbell curl, 12 kg (25 lb) dumbbells | 3 x 10-12 | 45-60 s |
| C2 | Triceps pushdown | 3 x 10-12 | 45-60 s |

The bench gets full rest. Everything else is paired, and each muscle still gets close to two minutes before its next set.

## How a rest timer keeps you consistent

The biggest rest-period mistake isn't picking 90 seconds instead of 2 minutes. It's not knowing how long you rested at all. Between conversations, picking a song and checking messages, a planned 2 minutes easily becomes 5.

A rest timer fixes this in three ways:

- **It removes guesswork.** You start the next set when the timer says, not when you remember.
- **It makes your log comparable.** Consistent rest means last week's numbers and this week's numbers were earned under the same conditions.
- **It frees your attention.** You can stretch, sip water or set up the next weight without watching a clock.

## Common rest-period mistakes

- **Using one rest time for everything.** A 60-second rest that's fine for lateral raises is too short for deadlifts.
- **Chasing fatigue.** Short rest feels harder, but "hard" isn't the same as productive. Losing two reps per set is lost training.
- **Resting too long on easy sets.** Five minutes between sets of calf raises usually just stretches your session out for no benefit.
- **Ignoring warm-up sets.** Light warm-ups need little rest. Save the full timer for your working sets.
- **Not adjusting on bad days.** If you slept poorly, an extra 30-60 seconds on compounds is a smart call, not a failure.

## Putting it into practice

Start simple. For your next few workouts, use these defaults:

- **Main compound lift:** 2.5-3 minutes (3-5 if you're working in sets of 1-5).
- **Secondary compound lifts:** 2 minutes.
- **Isolation exercises:** 60-90 seconds.

Keep these consistent for 3-4 weeks and watch your logs. If your reps fall off sharply from set 1 to set 3 on an exercise, add 30-60 seconds of rest there. If reps hold steady and you feel fully recovered well before the timer ends, you can trim it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is 1 minute rest between sets enough to build muscle?

One minute can build muscle, especially on isolation exercises like curls or lateral raises. For compound lifts it is usually too short, because you lose reps on later sets and total work drops. Research comparing 1 and 3 minutes in trained men found better strength and muscle gains with the longer rest. A good rule is at least 60-90 seconds for isolation and 2-3 minutes for big compound lifts.

### Should I rest longer between sets for strength or hypertrophy?

Rest longer for strength. Heavy sets of 1-5 reps rely on near-full recovery, so 3-5 minutes is common for squats, deadlifts and bench press. For hypertrophy in the 6-12 rep range, 2-3 minutes on compounds and 60-90 seconds on isolation exercises is a solid default. The type of exercise matters as much as the goal, so a heavy leg press needs more rest than a set of curls.

### How long should I rest between sets of squats vs curls?

For squats, rest about 2-3 minutes when training for muscle and 3-5 minutes when training heavy for strength. For curls, 60-90 seconds is usually plenty. Squats involve far more muscle and raise your heart rate and breathing much more, so they need longer to recover before you can match your previous set. Curls only fatigue a small muscle group, which recovers quickly.

### Do supersets reduce muscle gains?

Antagonist supersets, such as pairing curls with triceps pushdowns, generally let each muscle rest while the other works, so performance tends to hold up well while saving time. Supersets that combine exercises taxing the same muscles or heavy whole-body lifts can reduce reps and load. Keep your heaviest compound lifts as straight sets and use supersets mainly for accessory and isolation work.

### Is it bad to rest too long between sets?

Resting longer than you need is not harmful, but it makes sessions longer without much extra benefit. Recent research found little additional hypertrophy benefit beyond about 90 seconds on average, although heavy compound lifts often still benefit from 2-3 minutes or more. If you are consistently ready well before your timer ends on an exercise, it is reasonable to shorten rest there.
