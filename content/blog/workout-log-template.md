An **exercise workout log** is a record of every training session: the date, each exercise, how many sets you did, the reps and weight for each set, and a short note on how it felt. Keep it the same way every time and it tells you exactly what to lift next session. Below is a copy-ready template in kg and lb that works on paper, in Google Sheets or in an app, plus a three-week example and simple rules for reading it.

A log is the tool that makes [tracking progressive overload](/blog/how-to-track-progressive-overload) possible. You can't beat last week's numbers if you can't remember them, and "I think I did 60 for 8?" isn't a training plan.

## What is a workout log?

A workout log is the written history of what you actually lifted. It differs from a workout plan. The plan says what you intend to do ("bench press, 3 sets of 8 to 10"). The log says what happened ("60 kg x 10, 9, 8, last set slow"). You need both, but only the log tells you whether the plan is working.

A good log answers three questions in a few seconds:

1. **What did I do last time on this exercise?** This sets today's target.
2. **Am I getting stronger?** Compare the same exercise across four to eight weeks.
3. **Why did a session go badly?** Notes like "slept 5 hours" or "elbow sore" explain the dips so you don't overreact to them.

A notebook, a spreadsheet and an app are different ways of storing the same few numbers.

## What to record in an exercise workout log

Record six things for every working set. Anything beyond that is optional, and the more optional fields you add, the more likely you are to stop logging altogether.

| Field | What to write | Example (kg) | Example (lb) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Date** | The day you trained | 2 Dec 2026 | 2 Dec 2026 |
| **Exercise** | The exact same name every time | Barbell bench press | Barbell bench press |
| **Sets** | How many working sets | 3 | 3 |
| **Reps** | Reps per set, as performed | 10, 9, 8 | 10, 9, 8 |
| **Load** | Weight on the bar, or per dumbbell | 60 kg | 135 lb |
| **Notes** | How it felt, in a few words | "Last set slow, 1 rep left" | "Last set slow, 1 rep left" |

Four habits keep the log usable:

- **Write reps as performed, not as planned.** If you got 10, 9 and 8, write that. "3 x 10" when you didn't get it hides the very information you need next week.
- **Use one unit per exercise.** If your gym's plates are in kg, log in kg. Switching units halfway through a block makes the numbers impossible to compare at a glance.
- **Decide how you log dumbbells and stick to it.** Per hand is the usual convention. Our guide on [logging dumbbells per hand or total](/blog/log-dumbbells-per-hand-or-total) explains the options.
- **Keep notes short.** "Grindy", "easy, add weight", "knee niggle" and "bad sleep" are plenty.

### Optional fields

Add these only if you'll use them:

- **Body weight**, once a week, at the top of the page.
- **Rest time**, if you're deliberately shortening rest as a form of progress.
- **An effort rating**, such as reps left in the tank. "1 left" in the notes column does the same job without a new column.
- **Warm-up sets**, if you want a record of them. Mark them clearly so they never get mixed up with working sets.

## How to make a workout log on paper

Paper is the simplest option. There's nothing to set up or pay for, and a pen never needs an update. It does get slow to search once you've filled 40 pages.

![A clipboard with blank paper next to orange dumbbells and a yellow resistance band](/blog/workout-log-template/clipboard-dumbbells.webp)
*Photo: [Alesia Kozik](https://www.pexels.com/photo/clipboard-on-yellow-surface-8154203/) on Pexels*

### A notebook layout that works

1. **One session per page.** Write the date, the session name ("Upper A", "Push") and your body weight if you track it at the top.
2. **Plan before you train.** Before the session, flip back to the last time you did this workout and write today's targets under each exercise. It takes under a minute and saves you staring at the rack.
3. **Fill in as you go.** Write each set straight after you finish it, while you're resting. Memory at the end of a session is generous; it remembers 10 reps where you did 8.
4. **Circle the sets that hit the top of the range.** Next time you see a circle, you know that exercise is due for more weight.

### A free printable workout log

If you'd rather print than draw lines, paste the template above into a document, set the page to landscape, add 8 to 10 empty rows, and print a stack. Keep the sheets on a clipboard in your gym bag with a pen clipped to it. Twenty sheets covers about five weeks of four sessions a week.

The main downsides of paper: it's slow to compare exercises across months, there are no charts, and a lost notebook takes your history with it. Snap a photo of each full page if that worries you.

## How to set up a Google Sheets workout tracker

A spreadsheet is the middle ground. It costs nothing, it can do the maths for you, and it can draw charts. The trade-off is that typing into a spreadsheet on a phone between sets is fiddly, so many people log on paper or in a notes app and copy it into the sheet later.

### Column layout

The paper template keeps sets side by side. A spreadsheet works better with sets stacked in rows, because then one formula covers every row. Use one row per exercise per session when all sets are the same, and split the row when they aren't:

| Column | Header | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A | Date | 2026-12-02 |
| B | Exercise | Bench press |
| C | Sets | 2 |
| D | Reps | 10 |
| E | Load (kg) | 60 |
| F | Volume | `=C2*D2*E2` |
| G | Notes | Last set slow |

If you did 60 kg for 10, 10 and 8, use two rows: one with Sets = 2 and Reps = 10, and one with Sets = 1 and Reps = 8. That keeps every row exact.

### The volume formula

Volume is sets x reps x load. In cell F2, type:

`=C2*D2*E2`

Then drag the fill handle down the column. For 3 sets of 8 at 60 kg, the formula returns 1,440 kg. In pounds, 3 sets of 8 at 135 lb is 3,240 lb. Volume is most useful for comparing the same exercise week to week, not for comparing a squat with a curl.

### Three more formulas worth adding

- **Weekly volume for one exercise:** `=SUMIFS(F:F, B:B, "Bench press", A:A, ">="&DATE(2026,12,1), A:A, "<"&DATE(2026,12,8))` adds up all bench volume for the week starting 1 December.
- **Heaviest load ever on an exercise:** `=MAXIFS(E:E, B:B, "Back squat")` returns your top squat weight.
- **Number of sessions in a month:** `=COUNTUNIQUE(FILTER(A2:A, A2:A>=DATE(2026,12,1), A2:A<DATE(2027,1,1)))` counts the distinct training days in December.

### Small settings that save headaches

- **Freeze the header row** (View, then Freeze, then 1 row) so the column names stay visible as the sheet grows.
- **Use a dropdown for exercise names** (Data, then Data validation, then a dropdown from a list of your exercises). This stops "Bench", "bench press" and "BB bench" from splitting one exercise into three.
- **Make a chart per main lift.** Filter column B to one exercise, select the date and load columns, and insert a line chart.
- **Keep kg and lb apart.** If you train in two gyms with different plates, add a Unit column or keep a separate tab.

## Worked example: 3 weeks of an exercise workout log

Here's one lifter's "Full body A" session over three weeks. The squat uses straight sets of 5 with weight added each week. Bench press and the dumbbell row use [double progression](/blog/double-progression): stay at a weight until every set hits the top of the rep range, then add the smallest jump.

![A lifter gripping a loaded barbell on a rubber gym floor](/blog/workout-log-template/deadlift-setup.webp)
*Photo: [Victor Freitas](https://www.pexels.com/photo/close-up-of-a-person-s-lower-body-holding-barbell-949134/) on Pexels*

### In kilograms

| Week | Back squat (3 x 5) | Bench press (3 x 8-10) | DB row per hand (3 x 10-12) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 80 x 5, 5, 5 | 60 x 10, 9, 8 | 24 x 12, 11, 10 | Squat easy, 2 reps left |
| 2 | 82.5 x 5, 5, 5 | 60 x 10, 10, 9 | 24 x 12, 12, 11 | Good session |
| 3 | 85 x 5, 5, 4 | 60 x 10, 10, 10 | 24 x 12, 12, 12 | Slept 5 hours; missed last squat rep |
| 4 (plan) | 85 x 5, 5, 5 | 62.5 x 8+ | 26 x 10+ | Repeat squat, add weight to bench and row |

### In pounds

| Week | Back squat (3 x 5) | Bench press (3 x 8-10) | DB row per hand (3 x 10-12) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 175 x 5, 5, 5 | 135 x 10, 9, 8 | 50 x 12, 11, 10 | Squat easy, 2 reps left |
| 2 | 180 x 5, 5, 5 | 135 x 10, 10, 9 | 50 x 12, 12, 11 | Good session |
| 3 | 185 x 5, 5, 4 | 135 x 10, 10, 10 | 50 x 12, 12, 12 | Slept 5 hours; missed last squat rep |
| 4 (plan) | 185 x 5, 5, 5 | 140 x 8+ | 55 x 10+ | Repeat squat, add weight to bench and row |

### What the log shows

- **Bench press went up even though the weight didn't.** Volume rose from 1,620 kg to 1,740 kg to 1,800 kg (3,645 lb to 3,915 lb to 4,050 lb), all at 60 kg (135 lb). That's progress, and the log is the only place you'd see it.
- **The squat stall is probably about sleep.** The note explains the missed rep, so the plan is to repeat 85 kg (185 lb) rather than drop the weight or change the program.
- **The row is ready to move up.** All three sets hit 12, so next week's target is 26 kg (55 lb) per hand for at least 10 reps.

## How to read your log to choose next session's weights

Before each session, open the log to the last time you did each exercise and apply one of these rules. It takes about 30 seconds per exercise.

| What the log shows | What to do next session | Example (kg) | Example (lb) |
|---|---|---|---|
| All sets hit the top of the rep range | Add the smallest jump | 60 x 10, 10, 10 → 62.5 kg | 135 x 10, 10, 10 → 140 lb |
| Reps inside the range, not all at the top | Same weight, aim for one more rep | 60 x 10, 9, 8 → 60 kg, aim for 10, 10, 9 | 135 x 10, 9, 8 → 135 lb, aim for 10, 10, 9 |
| One set below the range, with a note explaining it | Repeat the same weight | 85 x 5, 5, 4 (bad sleep) → 85 kg | 185 x 5, 5, 4 (bad sleep) → 185 lb |
| Below the range two sessions in a row | Drop 5 to 10% and build back up | 85 x 4, 4, 3 twice → 77.5 to 80 kg | 185 x 4, 4, 3 twice → 170 to 175 lb |

Once a month, zoom out. Look at your best set on each main lift over the last four weeks. If it's going up, even slowly, leave the program alone. If it's been flat for three or four weeks, check sleep, food and missed sessions in your notes before you change anything.

## Common workout logging mistakes

Most logs fail for boring reasons.

1. **Changing exercise names.** "Incline DB press" in week 1 and "incline dumbbell bench" in week 3 look like two exercises. Pick one name and reuse it.
2. **Mixing kg and lb.** Seeing 60 one week and 135 the next makes your history unreadable at a glance. One unit per exercise, always.
3. **Switching between per hand and total for dumbbells.** 24 kg per hand and 48 kg total are the same lift. Logged inconsistently, they look like a 100% jump.
4. **Writing the plan instead of the result.** If the target was 3 x 10 and you got 10, 9, 8, write 10, 9, 8.
5. **Logging after the session from memory.** You'll round up. Write each set during the rest that follows it.
6. **Logging warm-ups as working sets.** A 40 kg (85 lb) warm-up set in the same column as your 80 kg (175 lb) work sets ruins the averages. Skip warm-ups or mark them "W".
7. **Never reading it.** A log you only write in is a diary. The value comes from the 30 seconds you spend reading it before each exercise.

## Putting it into practice

Pick a format for your exercise workout log today: a notebook, a printed sheet, a Google Sheet or an app. Copy the template above, set up your exercises with the same names you'll use every week, and log your next session set by set.

Before the session after that, read last time's numbers and write a target next to each exercise using the rules in the table. After three or four weeks you'll have a record that shows exactly where you're progressing and where you're stuck, which beats guessing every time you walk into the gym.

If you'd rather not draw tables, GymLedger turns the same template into fast set-by-set logging with a chart for each exercise. A notebook and a pen work just as well if you actually read them.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a workout log?

A workout log is a written record of what you did in each training session: the date, the exercises, the number of sets, the reps and weight for each set, and a short note on how it felt. It differs from a workout plan, which lists what you intend to do. The log shows whether you are progressing and what to lift next time.

### What should I write in my workout log?

For every working set, write the date, the exercise name, the reps you performed and the weight you used, plus a few words on how it felt. Keep exercise names and units identical from week to week. Body weight, rest times and warm-up sets are optional extras. Write reps as performed, such as 10, 9, 8, rather than the target you planned.

### How do I make a workout log in Google Sheets?

Create columns for Date, Exercise, Sets, Reps, Load, Volume and Notes, with one row per exercise per session. In the Volume column, use the formula =C2*D2*E2 to multiply sets, reps and load, then fill it down. Freeze the header row and use a dropdown for exercise names so the same lift is always spelled the same way.

### Is a paper workout log better than an app?

Neither is better in general. Paper is free, quick to start and never asks for a subscription, but it is slow to search and has no charts. An app pre-fills last session's numbers, runs a rest timer and charts every exercise. The best option is the one you will fill in during every session and read before every exercise.

### Should I log warm-up sets?

You don't have to. Most lifters log only working sets, because those are the sets that show progress. If you do record warm-ups, mark them clearly, for example with a W, so they never get mixed into your working-set averages or volume totals. Keeping warm-ups out of the main columns keeps your charts and comparisons accurate.
