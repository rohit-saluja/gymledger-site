Here's **how to load a barbell in kg**: subtract the bar's weight (usually 20 kg) from your target, divide what's left by two, and put that amount on each side using the biggest plates first. For 100 kg (220 lb) on a 20 kg Olympic bar, that's 80 kg of plates, or 40 kg per side: one 25 kg, one 15 kg. If you use competition collars, subtract 5 kg more before you split. The rest of this guide covers bar weights, plate colours, a full loading table, and how to handle lb plates and small jumps.

## Step one: know what your bar weighs

Every bit of plate math starts with the bar. Get this wrong and every number you log is off by the same amount, which quietly ruins your progress charts.

### The 20 kg Olympic bar

The standard men's Olympic bar weighs **20 kg (44 lb)**. Under International Weightlifting Federation specifications it's 2.2 m long, with 50 mm sleeves and a 28 mm grip, according to the summary on [Wikipedia's barbell page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbell). This is the bar you'll find in most squat racks and on most bench presses in a decently equipped gym. Powerlifting federations also use a 20 kg bar.

### The 15 kg women's Olympic bar

The women's Olympic bar weighs **15 kg (33 lb)**. It's shorter (about 2.01 m) with a thinner 25 mm grip and no centre knurling. Plenty of lifters of any gender prefer the thinner grip for Olympic lifts.

### Technique bars, EZ bars and fixed barbells

This is where people get caught out. Lighter **technique or training bars** exist in a range of weights, often well under 15 kg, and they're not standardised. **EZ curl bars** vary a lot between brands. **Fixed (pre-loaded) barbells** on a rack usually have the total printed on the end, but the bar itself isn't separately labelled.

The rule is simple: **if you don't know what a bar weighs, check.** Look for a stamp on the sleeve or end cap, ask the staff, or weigh it on a scale. Don't assume an EZ bar is 10 kg just because the last gym's was.

| Bar type | Typical weight | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Men's Olympic bar | 20 kg (44 lb) | Standard in most racks; also used in powerlifting |
| Women's Olympic bar | 15 kg (33 lb) | Thinner 25 mm grip |
| Technique / training bar | Varies | Check the marking or weigh it |
| EZ curl bar | Varies | Brands differ; check before logging |
| Fixed barbell | Total printed on end | Log the printed total |

## Step two: decide whether collars count

Collars hold the plates on. Whether you include their weight depends on what type you use.

- **Spring clips and light plastic lock collars** weigh very little. Most lifters ignore them, and that's fine as long as you always ignore them.
- **Competition collars** weigh **2.5 kg (5.5 lb) each**, so a pair adds **5 kg (11 lb)**. In powerlifting, the [IPF technical rules](https://www.powerlifting.sport/rules/codes/info/technical-rules) set up the bar so that it plus collars weighs 25 kg, as summarised on Wikipedia's barbell page.

If you train with competition collars, your starting weight is 25 kg, not 20 kg. The bar alone with collars is already 25 kg (55 lb).

## Step three: learn the kg plate colours

Calibrated kg plates follow a colour code set out by the IWF and widely used in powerlifting as well. Wikipedia's barbell page summarises it like this:

| Colour | Big plate | Small (change) plate |
|---|---|---|
| Red | 25 kg (55.1 lb) | 2.5 kg (5.5 lb) |
| Blue | 20 kg (44.1 lb) | 2 kg (4.4 lb) |
| Yellow | 15 kg (33.1 lb) | 1.5 kg (3.3 lb) |
| Green | 10 kg (22 lb) | 1 kg (2.2 lb) |
| White | 5 kg (11 lb) | 0.5 kg (1.1 lb) |

Two things to remember:

1. **Colour repeats.** Red is both 25 kg and 2.5 kg. Size tells you which is which, and the plate should be stamped with its weight.
2. **Your gym may not follow it.** Commercial gyms often use black iron or black bumper plates of every size. IPF-approved sets can use any colour for plates of 10 kg and below. Always read the number on the plate, not just the colour.

Most commercial gyms in kg also stock **1.25 kg (2.8 lb)** plates, which are handy for small jumps. Some have fractional plates of 0.5 kg or less.

## How to load a barbell in kg: the plate math

Here's the method that works every time.

1. **Start with your target total.** Example: 85 kg.
2. **Subtract the bar** (and collars, if they count). 85 − 20 = 65 kg of plates.
3. **Divide by two** to get the weight per side. 65 ÷ 2 = 32.5 kg per side.
4. **Fill each side from the largest plate down.** 25 kg fits (7.5 kg left). 5 kg fits (2.5 kg left). 2.5 kg fits (0 left).
5. **Load:** 25 + 5 + 2.5 per side.
6. **Double-check by adding it back up.** 20 + (32.5 × 2) = 85 kg (187.4 lb).

That's barbell plate math in kg. Once you've done it a few dozen times, you stop calculating and start recognising patterns.

### Worked example: 60 kg (132.3 lb)

- 60 − 20 = 40 kg of plates
- 40 ÷ 2 = **20 kg per side**
- One 20 kg plate each side (or 15 + 5 if the 20s are in use)

### Worked example: 102.5 kg (226 lb)

- 102.5 − 20 = 82.5 kg
- 82.5 ÷ 2 = **41.25 kg per side**
- 25 + 15 + 1.25 per side

This is the classic "I need a 2.5 kg jump" problem. Adding 2.5 kg to the total means **1.25 kg per side**, not 2.5.

### Worked example: 140 kg (308.6 lb) with competition collars

- 140 − 20 (bar) − 5 (two collars) = 115 kg
- 115 ÷ 2 = **57.5 kg per side**
- 25 + 25 + 5 + 2.5 per side

### Worked example: 47.5 kg (104.7 lb) on a 15 kg bar

- 47.5 − 15 = 32.5 kg
- 32.5 ÷ 2 = **16.25 kg per side**
- 15 + 1.25 per side

### Worked example: 72.5 kg (159.8 lb) with no 25s available

- 72.5 − 20 = 52.5 kg
- 52.5 ÷ 2 = **26.25 kg per side**
- 20 + 5 + 1.25 per side

Plenty of commercial gyms don't stock 25 kg or 15 kg plates. In that case, fill with 20s, then 10s and 5s.

## Converting between lb plates and kg

Many gyms mix kg bars with lb plates, or the other way round. Here's how to keep the numbers straight.

- **1 kg = 2.2046 lb**, so multiply kg by 2.2046 to get lb.
- **1 lb = 0.4536 kg**, so multiply lb by 0.4536 to get kg.

### Common lb plates in kg

| lb plate | kg equivalent |
|---|---|
| 45 lb | 20.41 kg |
| 35 lb | 15.88 kg |
| 25 lb | 11.34 kg |
| 10 lb | 4.54 kg |
| 5 lb | 2.27 kg |
| 2.5 lb | 1.13 kg |

### Worked example: lb plates on a 20 kg bar

Your gym's bar is a 20 kg Olympic bar but the plates are in lb. You load a 45 lb plate on each side.

- Plates: 45 × 2 = 90 lb = 40.82 kg
- Bar: 20 kg
- **Total: 60.82 kg (134.1 lb)**

Logging that as "60 kg" is close but not exact. Logging it as "135 lb" assumes a 45 lb bar, which is about 0.4 kg heavier than a 20 kg bar. Either way, pick one convention and stick to it.

### Worked example: 225 lb in kg

The classic "two plates" lift is 225 lb on a 45 lb bar. In kg terms, that's about **102.1 kg**. On kg equipment, the closest load is 102.5 kg (25 + 15 + 1.25 per side on a 20 kg bar).

### The practical rule

If the plates and bar are in different units, **log in the unit of the plates you can count most reliably**, and add the bar in the same unit. Consistency matters more than precision to the gram. ## Fractional plates: when 2.5 kg is too big a jump

A 2.5 kg jump (1.25 kg per side) is about 2.5% on a 100 kg squat. On a 40 kg overhead press, it's over 6%. For upper-body lifts and smaller lifters, that's a big step.

Fractional plates solve this. Sets commonly include **0.5 kg (1.1 lb)** and **0.25 kg (0.55 lb)** discs, and some go smaller. A pair of 0.5 kg plates adds 1 kg to the bar, which lets you go from 40 kg to 41 kg instead of 42.5 kg.

**How to use them:**

- Add one pair at a time, one plate per side.
- Put them on the outside, after your other plates.
- Track the exact total, such as 41 kg (90.4 lb), not "about 40."

If your gym doesn't have them, you can still progress by adding reps before weight. That's the idea behind [double progression](/blog/double-progression): hit the top of your rep range at one weight, then make the small jump.

## Safe loading and unloading order

Getting the numbers right is only half of how to load a barbell in kg safely. Loading a bar badly is how plates slide off, bars tip off racks, and toes get crushed.

### Loading

1. **Heaviest plates go on first, closest to the centre.** Then the next heaviest, then small plates last. This keeps the bar balanced and makes it easier to find the small plates when you change weight.
2. **Push each plate flush against the one before.** Gaps let plates shift.
3. **Alternate sides when the bar is in a rack.** Load one big plate on the left, then one on the right. Loading one side fully can tip the bar off the hooks.
4. **Put collars on before you lift.** Every set, every time.

### Unloading

1. **Remove collars.**
2. **Take plates off in the reverse order**, smallest first, alternating sides in a rack.
3. **Keep the bar level.** If a bar is on the floor, unloading one side completely is usually fine. In a rack or on bench hooks, it isn't.
4. **Return plates to the tree** so the next person can do their plate math without hunting.

### A quick safety check

Before your first rep, look at both ends. Same plates, same order, collars on. It takes two seconds and catches most loading mistakes. If you have an injury or aren't sure how much you should be lifting, check with a qualified coach or your doctor first.

## Log the total bar weight, consistently

Once the bar is loaded, write down the **total weight on the bar**, not the per-side number. "100 kg" is clear. "40 a side" leaves you guessing next month whether that was on a 20 kg or 15 kg bar, with or without collars.

A few rules that keep your history clean:

- **Include the bar.** Always log the total.
- **Decide on collars once.** If you count competition collars, always count them. If you ignore spring clips, always ignore them.
- **Note unusual bars.** If you used an EZ bar or a lighter technique bar, record the total including that bar's weight, once you've checked it.
- **Stay in one unit per lift.** Switching between kg and lb for the same exercise makes your progress charts jump around.

## Putting it into practice

Knowing how to load a barbell in kg comes down to the same five steps every time. Next time you're at the bar, run the routine:

1. Check the bar's weight. Assume nothing for unfamiliar bars.
2. Decide whether collars count.
3. Subtract, halve, and fill from the biggest plate down.
4. Load heaviest first, alternate sides in a rack, collars on.
5. Log the **total**, in the same unit every time.

Here's a quick test to lock it in. Work these out, then check:

- **75 kg (165.3 lb):** 27.5 per side = 25 + 2.5
- **117.5 kg (259 lb):** 48.75 per side = 25 + 20 + 2.5 + 1.25
- **62.5 kg (137.8 lb) on a 15 kg bar:** 23.75 per side = 20 + 2.5 + 1.25

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does an Olympic barbell weigh in kg?

A standard men's Olympic barbell weighs 20 kg (44 lb), and a women's Olympic bar weighs 15 kg (33 lb). Many gyms also have lighter technique bars, EZ curl bars and fixed barbells, and their weights vary by brand. If a bar isn't clearly marked, ask staff or weigh it before you start logging, so your totals stay accurate over time.

### How do I calculate plates per side in kg?

Subtract the bar weight from your target total, then divide by two. For example, 90 kg on a 20 kg bar leaves 70 kg of plates, which is 35 kg per side. Fill each side from the largest plate down, so 25 kg plus 10 kg. If you use 2.5 kg competition collars, subtract another 5 kg before dividing.

### Do collars count toward the weight on the bar?

Competition collars weigh 2.5 kg each, so a pair adds 5 kg and is normally counted. Spring clips and light plastic collars weigh very little, and most lifters ignore them. Either approach works as long as you're consistent. Pick a rule for your collars and apply it every session so your logged totals stay comparable.

### What colour is a 20 kg plate?

Under the IWF colour code, a 20 kg plate is blue. The full sequence is red 25 kg, blue 20 kg, yellow 15 kg, green 10 kg and white 5 kg, with the same colours repeating for the 2.5, 2, 1.5, 1 and 0.5 kg change plates. Many commercial gyms use black plates, so always read the stamped weight.

### Should I log the total weight or the weight per side?

Log the total weight on the bar, including the bar itself. A number like 100 kg is clear months later, while 40 per side depends on which bar and collars you used. Stay in one unit for each lift as well, since switching between kg and lb for the same exercise makes your progress harder to read.
