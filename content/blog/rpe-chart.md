An RPE chart for lifting rates each set by how many reps you had left when you stopped. RPE 10 means nothing left, RPE 9 means one rep left, RPE 8 means two, RPE 7 means three, and RPE 6 means four or more. In the gym, RPE (rating of perceived exertion) is shorthand for "how close to failure was that set?", and the number of reps you had left is called reps in reserve, or RIR.

Below is the full chart with half points, how it differs from the Borg scale used for cardio, how to use it to choose today's weight, an RPE to percentage chart with its limits, and worked sessions in kg and lb. If you want the bigger picture first, start with our pillar guide on [how to track progressive overload](/blog/how-to-track-progressive-overload). RPE is one way to decide what goes in that log.

## What RPE means in the gym

RPE started life as a cardio tool. Gunnar Borg's original scale rated whole-body effort during exercise, and lifters later borrowed the idea. The trouble is that "how hard did that feel?" is vague for a set of squats. Plenty of lifters call a set 8 out of 10 when they couldn't have done another rep.

The fix was to tie each number to reps in reserve. A 2016 study by Zourdos and colleagues tested a [resistance-training RPE scale built on reps in reserve](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26049792/), where RPE 10 means zero reps left, RPE 9 means one, and so on. A companion paper by Helms and colleagues, [on applying the RIR-based scale to training](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4961270/), notes that the same kind of scale first appeared in powerlifting coaching (the Reactive Training Systems manual) in 2008.

So when someone at the gym says "five at RPE 8", they mean five reps with about two left in the tank. That's the meaning this whole post uses.

## The RPE chart: RPE 6 to 10 with reps in reserve

This is the RPE chart most programs use. The half points are for sets where you're not sure between two numbers.

| RPE | Reps in reserve (RIR) | What it feels like | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **10** | 0 | Nothing left. Another rep would fail. | Last set of an isolation exercise, a planned max |
| **9.5** | 0 to 1 | Maybe one more, maybe not | Heavy singles and doubles |
| **9** | 1 | One more rep, definitely | Final sets, heavy triples |
| **8.5** | 1 to 2 | One more for sure, maybe two | Heavy working sets |
| **8** | 2 | Two more reps | Most working sets for strength and size |
| **7.5** | 2 to 3 | Two, probably three | First sets of the day, volume work |
| **7** | 3 | Three more, bar still moving well | Technique and volume sets |
| **6** | 4 or more | Solid effort, plenty left | Heavy warm-ups, deload weeks |
| **5 and below** | Many | Warm-up effort | Warm-ups |

In the version of the scale described by Helms and colleagues, scores of 5 to 6 cover roughly 4 to 6 reps in reserve, 3 to 4 means light effort, and 1 to 2 means little to no effort. Below RPE 6, nobody can tell three reps left from six, so don't pretend to.

### RPE vs reps in reserve: same thing, two labels

RPE and RIR are two ways of writing the same rating: **RPE = 10 minus RIR**. "3 x 8 at RPE 8" and "3 x 8 at 2 RIR" mean the same session. Some programs use one, some the other. Pick whichever your program speaks and stick with it in your notes.

## RPE vs the Borg scale for cardio

If you've had an RPE scale shown to you by a physio or on a treadmill poster, it was probably the Borg scale. It measures something different.

![Man riding a stationary exercise bike in a gym](/blog/rpe-chart/exercise-bike.webp)
*Photo: [Ivan S](https://www.pexels.com/photo/man-on-an-exercise-bike-at-the-gym-4162580/) on Pexels*

| Scale | Range | What you rate | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Borg RPE** | 6 to 20 | Whole-body effort during steady exercise; the numbers were chosen to roughly track heart rate | Cardio, rehab, health checks |
| **Borg CR10 (modified)** | 0 to 10 | Effort, judged mostly by breathing | Cardio and general exercise |
| **RIR-based RPE** | 1 to 10 (mostly 6 to 10) | Reps left at the end of one set | Lifting |

Cleveland Clinic's [explainer on the RPE scale](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/17450-rated-perceived-exertion-rpe-scale) covers the two Borg versions: 6 is no exertion and 20 is maximum on the original, while the 0 to 10 version is judged mainly by how hard you're breathing.

In practice, RPE 8 on a bike means minutes of hard breathing. RPE 8 on a squat means you racked the bar with two reps left, and you might be breathing hard for a different reason entirely. Don't use one scale's numbers on the other.

## How to use an RPE chart to pick today's weight

Percentage programs tell you to lift 80 percent of your max whatever kind of day you're having. RPE lets the weight move with you. Helms and colleagues point out that strength varies with sleep, food and stress, and a max tested weeks ago may not match today.

Here's the process for a session that says **3 x 5 at RPE 8**.

1. **Start from your log.** Last week you squatted 100 kg (225 lb) for 5 and it felt like an 8.
2. **Warm up and pay attention.** Bar, then 60 kg, 80 kg, 90 kg x 2 (or 135, 175, 200 lb). If 90 kg feels like a heavy single, today is not the day for a jump.
3. **Do the first working set at last week's weight** (or a small jump if warm-ups flew). Rate it honestly the moment you rack it.
4. **Adjust the next set.** At 3 to 6 reps, one RPE point is worth about 2.5 to 3 percent of your max, based on the percentage chart below. On a squat around 100 kg (225 lb), that's one small jump: 2.5 kg (5 lb).

| Set | kg | lb | RPE called | Next set |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 x 5 | 225 x 5 | 9 | Drop 2.5 kg / 5 lb |
| 2 | 97.5 x 5 | 220 x 5 | 8 | Keep |
| 3 | 97.5 x 5 | 220 x 5 | 8.5 | Done |

That's a normal "meh" day, handled in one adjustment. On a good day, set 1 at 100 kg (225 lb) feels like a 7, and you go up 2.5 kg (5 lb) for set 2.

## RPE to percentage of 1RM chart

Many lifters want to turn an RPE target into a starting weight. That needs a second RPE chart, one that maps reps and RPE to a percentage of your one-rep max (1RM). The values below are the widely used powerlifting chart associated with Mike Tuchscherer of Reactive Training Systems, as listed by [rpetraining.com](https://rpetraining.com/rpe-to-percent).

| Reps | RPE 10 | RPE 9 | RPE 8 | RPE 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100% | 95.5% | 92.2% | 89.2% |
| 2 | 95.5% | 92.2% | 89.2% | 86.3% |
| 3 | 92.2% | 89.2% | 86.3% | 83.7% |
| 4 | 89.2% | 86.3% | 83.7% | 81.1% |
| 5 | 86.3% | 83.7% | 81.1% | 78.6% |
| 6 | 83.7% | 81.1% | 78.6% | 76.2% |

Read it along the diagonal. Six reps at RPE 10, five at RPE 9, four at RPE 8 and three at RPE 7 are all about 84 percent. Helms and colleagues make the same point using their own chart: roughly 83 percent of 1RM is a 6-rep max, so those combinations are about the same load.

### Worked example: from chart to plates

Say your squat max is 120 kg (265 lb). Multiply by the chart value, then round to what you can load.

| Target | Chart | kg | Load (kg) | lb | Load (lb) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 @ RPE 8 | 81.1% | 97.3 | 97.5 | 214.9 | 215 |
| 3 @ RPE 9 | 89.2% | 107.0 | 107.5 | 236.4 | 235 |
| 1 @ RPE 8 | 92.2% | 110.6 | 110 | 244.3 | 245 |

### Why this chart is a starting point, not a rule

Helms and colleagues are blunt about the limits of their version: it was built from average ratings of trained lifters on the back squat, and they say it shouldn't be treated as an absolute conversion tool. People differ in how many reps they can do at the same percentage, and the relationship may shift for bench, machines or single-joint lifts.

Tuchscherer himself suggests [customising the chart for each lift](https://store.reactivetrainingsystems.com/blogs/advanced-concepts/customizing-your-rpe-chart) by testing a 1RM, 4RM and 8RM and filling in the gaps. If you don't want to test, use the chart for the first set, then let the RPE you actually feel steer the rest.

## How beginners can learn RPE

Judging reps in reserve is a skill, and the research says most people start out a bit off. A 2022 meta-analysis found lifters [underpredicted how many reps they had left by about one rep on average](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/), with big differences between people. Accuracy improved closer to failure and in sets of 12 reps or fewer. Training experience didn't seem to change it much.

Distance from failure matters a lot. In one study of trained squatters doing a set to failure at 70 percent of their max (about 16 reps on average), [predictions were off by about 2 reps at a called RPE 9, but about 5 reps at a called RPE 5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30747900/). Close to failure, people are decent. Far from it, they're guessing.

Practice does help, given time. In a small study of powerlifters picking their own loads, [ratings landed within about a third of an RPE point of the target on average](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28933716/), and accuracy on lighter, faster squat sets took about three weeks to peak.

Here's how to learn it without wrecking your progress:

1. **Rate every working set now, but don't train by it yet.** Helms and colleagues suggest novices record ratings alongside normal training until they're accurate. Keep following your plan's weights and reps for the first couple of months.
2. **Calibrate on safe exercises.** Every few weeks, on a leg press, machine chest press or dumbbell curl, call your reps in reserve at some point in the last set, then keep going to failure. If you said "2 left" and got 5, you've learned something.
3. **Learn on sets of 5 to 12 reps.** Ratings are more accurate there than on sets of 15 to 20.
4. **Watch bar speed.** Zourdos and colleagues found a strong link between slower reps and higher RPE. The rep that slows down noticeably is usually your cue that the set is getting serious.
5. **Use safeties or a spotter** on squats and bench when you test how many reps you really have.

Write the number down. GymLedger records the weight and reps for every set and draws a strength chart for each exercise. Your effort rating goes on paper or in your head, and that's enough to see whether the same weight gets easier over the weeks.

## Common RPE mistakes

RPE is honest-system training. That's its strength and its main weakness.

![Lifter gripping a loaded barbell before a deadlift](/blog/rpe-chart/deadlift.webp)
*Photo: [Victor Freitas](https://www.pexels.com/photo/man-holding-barbell-841125/) on Pexels*

### Sandbagging

Calling a set RPE 8 when it was really a 6, usually because failure feels scary. The meta-analysis above suggests this is the default error: most lifters think they're closer to failure than they are. The result is months of sets that feel productive and aren't. Calibration sets fix it fast.

### Ego ratings

The opposite problem. A grinder with a hitched lockout gets logged as "8, felt fine", so next week's weight goes up. If the last rep slowed to a crawl, it was a 9.5 or a 10. Rate the rep, not the mood.

### Rating the session instead of the set

"Today felt like an 8" is a session rating. It's useful for spotting fatigue, but the RPE chart above rates one set at a time. Rate each set the moment you rack the weight, before rest and adrenaline rewrite history.

### Treating RPE as permission to skip progression

Autoregulation is supposed to move the weight both ways. If the same 100 kg (225 lb) has been "RPE 8" for six weeks, either you aren't getting stronger or your ratings are drifting. Same weight, same reps and a lower RPE than last month is progress, and it's your cue to add load.

### Changing the lift to hit the number

Cutting depth on squats or bouncing the bench to keep a set at RPE 8 makes the number meaningless. Keep a fixed technique standard and let the weight change instead.

## Worked example sessions in kg and lb

Three sessions show how RPE plays out across weeks and on bad days.

### Session A: squat top set and back-offs over four weeks

Plan: one top set of 5 at RPE 8, then 3 x 5 at about 90 percent of the top set.

| Week | Top set (kg) | RPE | Back-offs (kg) | Top set (lb) | Back-offs (lb) | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 x 5 | 7.5 | 90 x 5, 5, 5 | 225 x 5 | 200 x 5, 5, 5 | Easier than planned: add |
| 2 | 102.5 x 5 | 8 | 92.5 x 5, 5, 5 | 230 x 5 | 205 x 5, 5, 5 | On target: add |
| 3 | 105 x 5 | 9 | 92.5 x 5, 5, 5 | 235 x 5 | 205 x 5, 5, 5 | Too hard: back-offs held, repeat top set |
| 4 | 105 x 5 | 8 | 95 x 5, 5, 5 | 235 x 5 | 210 x 5, 5, 5 | Same weight, lower RPE: add next week |

Week 4 is the important row. The weight didn't change, but the same 105 kg (235 lb) dropped from RPE 9 to RPE 8. That's progress you'd miss if you only looked at the load.

### Session B: a bad-day bench

Plan: 4 x 6 at RPE 7 to 8, starting from last week's 80 kg (175 lb). The warm-up single at 70 kg (155 lb) felt heavy, which is a hint.

| Set | kg | lb | RPE called | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 80 x 6 | 175 x 6 | 9 | Drop 2.5 kg / 5 lb |
| 2 | 77.5 x 6 | 170 x 6 | 8 | Keep |
| 3 | 77.5 x 6 | 170 x 6 | 8 | Keep |
| 4 | 77.5 x 6 | 170 x 6 | 8.5 | Done |

All 24 reps got done in the right effort zone. A percentage program would have had you grinding 80 kg (175 lb) for four sets, probably missing reps on the last two.

### Session C: hypertrophy accessory

Plan: one-arm dumbbell row, 3 sets of 10 to 12 per arm, the first two at RPE 8 and the last at RPE 9 to 10.

| Set | kg (per hand) | lb (per hand) | Reps | RPE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 30 | 65 | 12 | 8 |
| 2 | 30 | 65 | 11 | 8.5 |
| 3 | 30 | 65 | 11 | 10 |

Next session, aim for 12 on all three sets at RPE 8 to 9, then move up a dumbbell. RPE here simply makes sure the sets are hard enough to count. For more week-by-week progressions like this, see our [progressive overload examples](/blog/progressive-overload-examples).

## What RPE to train at, and when it signals a deload

For most lifters, most working sets belong around **RPE 7 to 9**: one to three reps left.

- **For muscle size**, a 2024 set of meta-regressions found [muscle growth improved as sets were taken closer to failure](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/), while strength gains were similar across a wide range of reps in reserve.
- **Failure isn't required.** A 2023 meta-analysis found [no clear advantage for training to momentary failure](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334240/) over stopping short for muscle growth.
- **Practical split.** Helms and colleagues suggest keeping main lifts like squats and bench mostly at RPE 6 to 8, and saving RPE 10 for the last set of simple isolation exercises.

RPE also helps you count volume. A set at RPE 5 doesn't do much for growth, so when you add up [how many sets per muscle group per week](/blog/how-many-sets-per-muscle-group-per-week) you're doing, count the sets at roughly RPE 7 and above.

And RPE is an early fatigue warning. If familiar weights feel one to two points harder than usual across several lifts for a week or two, and sleep and food are fine, it's probably time for a [deload week](/blog/deload-week).

## Putting it into practice

1. **Learn the chart:** RPE 10 is no reps left, 9 is one, 8 is two, 7 is three, 6 is four or more.
2. **Rate every working set** for a few weeks before you use RPE to choose weights.
3. **Calibrate** with a set to failure on a safe exercise every few weeks.
4. **Use RPE to adjust**, roughly 2.5 percent per point, not to skip progression.
5. **Aim most sets at RPE 7 to 9.** Save RPE 10 for the last set of isolation work.
6. **Keep your log simple.** Weight and reps for every set, with the RPE beside them on paper.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does RPE mean in the gym?

RPE stands for rating of perceived exertion. In lifting, it describes how close a set was to failure on a 1 to 10 scale, tied to reps in reserve. RPE 10 means you could not have done another rep, RPE 9 means one rep was left, and RPE 8 means two were left. It lets programs prescribe effort instead of a fixed weight, so the load can change with how strong you are that day.

### What is RPE 8 in lifting?

RPE 8 means you finished the set with about two reps left in reserve. If a program says 5 reps at RPE 8, pick a weight you could lift about 7 times, and stop at 5. It is the most common target for working sets because it is hard enough to drive progress while leaving room to keep quality high across several sets.

### Is RPE the same as RIR?

They describe the same thing in different units. Reps in reserve counts the reps you had left, and RPE converts that onto a 10-point scale, so RPE equals 10 minus RIR. RPE 8 is 2 RIR, and RPE 9 is 1 RIR. Some programs prefer RIR because it is easier for beginners to picture. Use whichever your program uses and stay consistent.

### What RPE should I train at to build muscle?

Most working sets should land around RPE 7 to 9, meaning one to three reps left. Research suggests muscle growth improves as sets get closer to failure, but training all the way to failure does not show a clear advantage. Keep big lifts like squats and bench mostly at RPE 7 to 8, and push the last set of isolation exercises to RPE 9 or 10.

### How can a beginner learn to rate RPE accurately?

Rate every working set for a few weeks without using the number to choose weights. Every few weeks, on a safe machine or dumbbell exercise, call how many reps you have left partway through the last set, then continue to failure and compare. Learn on sets of 5 to 12 reps, since ratings are more accurate there than on very high-rep sets.

### Can I use the RPE chart for cardio?

Not this one. The lifting RPE chart is based on reps left in a set, which has no meaning on a run or a bike. For cardio, use the Borg scale, either the original 6 to 20 version or the 0 to 10 version, which rate overall effort and breathing during steady exercise. The two scales use similar numbers but measure different things.
