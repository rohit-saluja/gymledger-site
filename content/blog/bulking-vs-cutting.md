Cutting vs bulking is a choice between eating more than you burn to build muscle (bulking) and eating less than you burn to lose fat while keeping your muscle (cutting). If you're fairly lean and want more size, bulk. If you're carrying more fat than you're happy with, cut first. If you're new to lifting or somewhere in between, eating at maintenance is a real third option. Whichever you choose, go slowly: gain about **0.25-0.5% of body weight a week** on a bulk, or lose about **0.5-1% a week** on a cut.

Below you'll find a decision table, calorie targets with worked examples in kg and lb, how long each phase should last, and what to track so you know it's working.

## What is bulking and cutting?

**Bulking** means eating in a planned calorie surplus while training hard. You accept a little fat gain because the extra food makes building muscle easier. **Cutting** means eating in a planned deficit while still lifting heavy and keeping protein high, so the weight you lose comes mostly from fat. **Maintenance** sits between them: you eat roughly what you burn and your weight holds steady.

| | Bulk | Cut | Maintenance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calories | About 10-20% above maintenance | Below maintenance, set by your target rate | About maintenance |
| Weekly weight change | +0.25-0.5% of body weight | -0.5-1% of body weight | Roughly flat |
| Protein | 1.6-2.2 g per kg | Higher, especially when lean | 1.6-2.2 g per kg |
| Training | Push for more reps and weight | Keep the heavy loads | Push for more reps and weight |
| Typical length | Several months | About 6 weeks to 4 months | As long as you like |

Protein deserves its own guide, and it has one. For grams per day at every body weight and how the target rises on a cut, see [how much protein to build muscle](/blog/how-much-protein-to-build-muscle).

## Cutting vs bulking: which should you do first?

Three things settle it: how much body fat you carry, how long you've been training, and what you want in the next six months.

### Body fat, described in words

Body-fat percentages from bathroom scales and mirror guesses are rough, so it's more useful to describe where you are:

- **Lean.** You can see an outline of your abs in good light, and your waist looks small next to your shoulders.
- **In between.** No visible abs, a bit of softness at the waist, but your clothes fit fine and you're not unhappy with how you look.
- **Carrying more fat than you'd like.** Your waist is the first thing to grow, your belt has moved a notch or two, and losing fat is the main thing on your mind.
- **Slim or underweight.** Clothes hang off you and keeping weight on is a struggle.
- **Skinny-fat.** You look slim in clothes but soft at the waist, with little visible muscle.

If you want numbers, coach Andy Morgan at [RippedBody](https://rippedbody.com/cut-or-bulk/) suggests that lifters past the beginner stage keep their cut-and-bulk cycles roughly within 10-20% body fat for men (add about 8 points for women), and stop bulking at around 20%. At [MacroFactor](https://macrofactor.com/bulk-or-cut/), Greg Nuckols suggests men might consider cutting for health reasons above about 25% body fat and women above about 35%. Treat both as rough guides, since the measurement itself is rough.

### Training age

How long you've been lifting changes the maths. In your first year of consistent training, while you're still adding weight to the bar most weeks, you can build muscle at maintenance or even in a small deficit. That's why beginners often do well without a formal bulk.

After that, muscle comes slowly. Nuckols estimates that drug-free men gain about 20-30 lb (9-14 kg) of fat-free mass over their whole training life and women about 12-20 lb (5-9 kg), with most of it arriving in the first year. Once the easy gains are gone, a surplus makes the remaining progress noticeably easier.

### Your goals

- **More size and strength over the long run:** bulk.
- **A weight class, a wedding or a holiday on a fixed date:** cut, timed backwards from the date.
- **Health:** if your body fat is high, cutting first is the sensible call.
- **Burned out after a long bulk:** a short cut or a few weeks at maintenance gives you a break from eating so much.
- **Genuinely torn:** Nuckols' advice is to pick whichever phase excites you more, since you'll stick with it longer.

### Decision table

| Your situation | Start with | Why |
|---|---|---|
| New to lifting, normal weight | Maintenance, or a small surplus | Beginners build muscle well without a big surplus |
| New to lifting, slim or underweight | Lean bulk | You have room to gain, and extra food speeds up early progress |
| New to lifting, carrying a lot of fat | Cut | Fat loss is the priority, and beginners can still build some muscle in a deficit |
| Skinny-fat | Maintenance, a very small surplus, or a slow cut | RippedBody suggests a slight surplus (about 1% of body weight a month) on the leaner side and a cut slightly slower than 0.5% a week on the softer side |
| A year or more of training, lean, want size | Lean bulk | Muscle comes slowly now, and a surplus helps |
| A year or more of training, above about 20% (men) or 28% (women) | Cut | Gets you back into a range where a bulk can run for months |
| Weight class or event coming up | Cut | Time it so you reach the target a week or two early |
| Just finished a long bulk and sick of eating | Short cut or maintenance | A break before the next push |
| Can't decide | Maintenance for 4-8 weeks | Train hard, track, then choose with better data |

## Calorie surplus for bulking: how big and how fast

A [2019 review of off-season nutrition for bodybuilders by Iraki and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31247944/) recommends a **surplus of about 10-20%** above maintenance, aiming to gain **about 0.25-0.5% of body weight per week** for novice and intermediate lifters. Advanced lifters should be more conservative with both.

### Worked example: setting the surplus

Say you're 75 kg (165 lb) and you maintain your weight on about 2,600 kcal a day.

1. 10% surplus: 2,600 × 1.10 = **2,860 kcal**
2. 20% surplus: 2,600 × 1.20 = **3,120 kcal**
3. Start at the low end, around 2,850 kcal, and let the scale tell you whether to add more.

### Target weekly gain by body weight

| Body weight | Slower (0.25% a week) | Faster (0.5% a week) | Over 12 weeks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 kg (132 lb) | 0.15 kg (0.33 lb) | 0.30 kg (0.66 lb) | 1.8-3.6 kg (4-8 lb) |
| 70 kg (154 lb) | 0.18 kg (0.39 lb) | 0.35 kg (0.77 lb) | 2.1-4.2 kg (4.6-9.3 lb) |
| 80 kg (176 lb) | 0.20 kg (0.44 lb) | 0.40 kg (0.88 lb) | 2.4-4.8 kg (5.3-10.6 lb) |
| 90 kg (198 lb) | 0.23 kg (0.50 lb) | 0.45 kg (0.99 lb) | 2.7-5.4 kg (6-11.9 lb) |
| 100 kg (220 lb) | 0.25 kg (0.55 lb) | 0.50 kg (1.1 lb) | 3-6 kg (6.6-13.2 lb) |

Newer lifters can aim for the faster column. If you've been training for a few years, the slower column, or even a little under it, is the better target. A faster gain at that stage mostly means more fat to cut later.

### Adjusting as you go

Calorie estimates are only a starting point, because your real maintenance is never exactly what a calculator says. Use the scale trend instead. If your weekly average hasn't moved for two or three weeks, add 100-200 kcal a day. If you're gaining well above your target rate, take 100-200 kcal away.

Don't know your maintenance? Log what you eat and weigh yourself every morning for two weeks without changing anything. If the weekly average holds steady, the calories you ate are your maintenance.

## Calorie deficit for cutting: how big and how fast

A review of [evidence-based contest preparation by Helms and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864135/) recommends setting calories so you lose **about 0.5-1% of body weight per week** to keep as much muscle as possible. Lean lifters should stay near 0.5%; people with more fat to lose can move faster.

| Body weight | Steady (0.5% a week) | Faster (1% a week) | 12 weeks at 0.5% |
|---|---|---|---|
| 70 kg (154 lb) | 0.35 kg (0.77 lb) | 0.70 kg (1.5 lb) | 4.2 kg (9.3 lb) |
| 80 kg (176 lb) | 0.40 kg (0.88 lb) | 0.80 kg (1.8 lb) | 4.8 kg (10.6 lb) |
| 90 kg (198 lb) | 0.45 kg (0.99 lb) | 0.90 kg (2 lb) | 5.4 kg (11.9 lb) |
| 100 kg (220 lb) | 0.50 kg (1.1 lb) | 1.0 kg (2.2 lb) | 6 kg (13.2 lb) |

In calories, that usually means eating about 250-500 kcal a day below maintenance. An 80 kg (176 lb) lifter who maintains on 2,700 kcal would start somewhere between 2,200 and 2,450 kcal, then adjust from the weekly average after two or three weeks.

Two things change on a cut. Protein goes up, because the lower your calories and the leaner you get, the more protein it takes to hold on to muscle; the [protein guide](/blog/how-much-protein-to-build-muscle) has the numbers. And your lifts become your early-warning system: if most of them start dropping, the deficit is probably too steep.

If you have a lot of weight to lose, a medical condition, or a history of disordered eating, talk to a doctor or registered dietitian before you start. Helms and colleagues specifically flag the higher risk of eating and body-image problems in physique sports.

## Lean bulk vs dirty bulk

A **dirty bulk** means eating everything in sight and watching the scale climb. It works, in the sense that you get bigger. Most of the extra size is fat, though, and every kilo of fat you gain is one you'll have to cut later. The Cleveland Clinic's advice on [bulking vs cutting](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/bulking-vs-cutting) warns against it for exactly that reason.

A **lean bulk** keeps the surplus small and the rate of gain inside the ranges above. It's slower on the scale and faster overall, because you spend fewer months cutting.

| | Lean bulk | Dirty bulk |
|---|---|---|
| Surplus | About 10-20% | Whatever you can eat |
| Weekly gain | 0.25-0.5% of body weight | Often 1% or more |
| Food | Mostly whole food, planned meals | Anything with calories |
| What you gain | Muscle plus a little fat | Muscle plus a lot of fat |
| What comes next | A short cut, or none | A long cut |

### What a lean bulking diet looks like

The Iraki review gives a simple framework: protein at **1.6-2.2 g per kg**, fat at **0.5-1.5 g per kg**, and the rest from carbohydrate (at least 3-5 g per kg to fuel training).

Here's that worked out for a 75 kg (165 lb) lifter eating 2,900 kcal:

1. **Protein:** 1.8 g × 75 = 135 g, or 540 kcal (4 kcal per gram)
2. **Fat:** 1.0 g × 75 = 75 g, or 675 kcal (9 kcal per gram)
3. **Carbohydrate:** 2,900 − 540 − 675 = 1,685 kcal, which is about 420 g (5.6 g per kg)

Most of the extra calories on a lean bulking diet should come from food that fuels training: rice, roti, oats, potatoes, fruit, milk. The surplus can be small. A cup of whole milk (about 150 kcal) and 30 g of peanuts (about 170 kcal) adds roughly 320 kcal, using values from [USDA FoodData Central](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/). Add that to a maintenance day and you're in a lean-bulk surplus without a single dirty-bulk pizza.

## How long a bulk or cut should last

There's no fixed answer, but the ranges are fairly consistent.

**Bulks run for months.** The Cleveland Clinic suggests four to eight months, especially for a first bulk. RippedBody's Andy Morgan recommends at least five to six months per bulk. A bulk that lasts six weeks barely gets going before it ends. Stop when you reach the top of the body-fat range you're comfortable with, or when your waist is growing faster than your lifts.

**Cuts are shorter.** The Cleveland Clinic suggests roughly six weeks to four months. Work it out backwards from how much you want to lose:

| Goal | Rate | Roughly how long |
|---|---|---|
| Lose 4 kg (9 lb) from 80 kg (176 lb) | 0.5% a week, 0.4 kg (0.9 lb) | 10 weeks |
| Lose 8 kg (18 lb) from 90 kg (198 lb) | 0.5-1% a week, 0.45-0.9 kg (1-2 lb) | 9-18 weeks |
| Gain 4 kg (9 lb) from 70 kg (154 lb) | 0.25-0.5% a week, 0.18-0.35 kg (0.4-0.8 lb) | 11-23 weeks |

If a cut needs more than about four months, consider splitting it in two with a few weeks at maintenance in the middle. After any cut, a few weeks at maintenance before you bulk lets your weight settle and shows you where your new maintenance is.

## What to track during a bulk or cut

Four numbers tell you almost everything. You don't need body-fat scans.

| What | How | Bulk working | Cut working | Red flag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Scale trend | Weigh each morning, average each week | Rising 0.25-0.5% a week | Falling 0.5-1% a week | Flat for three weeks, or far faster than planned |
| Waist | Tape at the navel, same morning each week | Rising slowly | Falling | On a bulk, waist rising quickly while lifts stall |
| Lifts | Top sets in your training log | Going up | Holding steady | On a cut, dropping across most lifts |
| Protein | Daily intake against your target | Around 1.6-2.2 g per kg | Around 2 g per kg or more | Regularly well under target |

**Scale trend.** Single weigh-ins swing by a kilo or more for reasons that have nothing to do with fat. The weekly average is what counts; our guide on [how often to weigh yourself](/blog/how-often-to-weigh-yourself) shows how to calculate and read it.

**Waist.** On a bulk, the waist tells you what kind of weight you're gaining. A slowly rising scale with a steady waist is mostly good news. A waist that grows a centimetre a week while your bench press sits still means the surplus is too big.

**Lifts.** On a bulk your main lifts should trend up; on a cut, holding them is a win. Log every session so you're comparing real numbers, not memories. [How to track progressive overload](/blog/how-to-track-progressive-overload) covers what to look for. GymLedger draws a strength chart for every exercise next to your body-weight chart, so you can see both lines on the same phone.

**Protein.** It's the one food number worth tracking even if you don't count anything else. In GymLedger's Diet tab you type what you ate in plain words and AI estimates calories, protein, carbs and fat. Your protein target is set from body weight at 1.6 g per kg, and the dashboard shows today's intake against it, plus how your week is going. On a cut you'll want to eat above that line.

## Maintenance and recomposition: the third option

The cutting vs bulking debate tends to skip the option that suits a lot of people best: eating at maintenance. Nuckols calls time at maintenance "a great default option". With enough protein and progressive training, you can lose a little fat and gain a little muscle while your weight barely moves.

That slow swap is called recomposition, and it works best for:

- **Beginners**, who build muscle quickly whatever their calories
- **People returning after a long break**, who regain old muscle fast
- **Skinny-fat lifters**, who need more muscle and less fat in roughly equal measure

The downside is that progress is hard to see on the scale. Your waist and your training log have to do the talking. For how to set it up, including protein targets, see our guide to [body recomposition](/blog/body-recomposition).

Maintenance also earns a place between phases. A few weeks there after a cut, or after a long bulk, gives your appetite and your head a break before the next push.

## Putting it into practice

1. **Pick your phase** using the decision table. When in doubt, start at maintenance for four to eight weeks.
2. **Find your maintenance calories** by logging food and morning weight for two weeks.
3. **Set your target.** Bulk: 10-20% above maintenance, aiming for 0.25-0.5% gain a week. Cut: about 250-500 kcal below, aiming for 0.5-1% loss a week.
4. **Set protein** at 1.6-2.2 g per kg, higher on a cut.
5. **Track four things:** weekly average weight, waist, lifts and protein.
6. **Adjust by 100-200 kcal** only after two or three weeks off target.
7. **End the phase** when you reach your body-fat limit (bulk), your goal (cut), or when the red flags in the tracking table show up.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should a beginner bulk or cut first?

It depends on where you start. A beginner at a normal weight can usually build muscle at maintenance or in a small surplus, so there's no need to pick an extreme. If you're slim or underweight, a lean bulk makes sense. If you carry a lot of fat, cut first; beginners can still build some muscle in a deficit while the fat comes off.

### How big should a calorie surplus for bulking be?

A surplus of about 10-20% above maintenance works for most lifters. For someone maintaining on 2,600 kcal, that's roughly 2,860-3,120 kcal a day. Start at the low end and aim to gain about 0.25-0.5% of body weight a week. If your weekly average weight doesn't move for two or three weeks, add 100-200 kcal and check again.

### How fast should I lose weight on a cut?

About 0.5-1% of body weight per week is a good target for keeping muscle. For an 80 kg (176 lb) lifter, that's 0.4-0.8 kg (0.9-1.8 lb) a week. Leaner lifters should stay near 0.5%. If your lifts start dropping across the board, slow down. Check with a doctor first if you have a lot to lose or a medical condition.

### Can you bulk and cut at the same time?

Sort of. Eating at maintenance with enough protein and progressive training can lead to slow fat loss and muscle gain at the same time, called body recomposition. It works best for beginners, people returning after a break, and those with more fat to lose. Experienced, lean lifters usually make faster progress with separate bulking and cutting phases.

### How long should a bulk last?

Plan on months rather than weeks. Common advice ranges from about four to eight months, and some coaches suggest six months or more for experienced lifters. End the bulk when you reach the top of the body-fat range you're comfortable with, or when your waist starts growing faster than your lifts improve.

### Is dirty bulking ever worth it?

Rarely. A dirty bulk adds weight quickly, but much of it is fat, which means a longer cut afterwards. A lean bulk with a 10-20% surplus and a steady weekly gain usually ends with a similar amount of muscle and far less fat to lose. The exception might be someone very underweight who struggles to eat enough of anything.
