If you are wondering how to log dumbbell weight per hand or total, the short answer is: **log the weight of one dumbbell**. If you press a pair of 24 kg (50 lb) dumbbells, write 24 kg (50 lb), not 48 kg (100 lb). It matches the number stamped on the dumbbell, it works the same for one-arm and two-arm exercises, and it is what most lifters and coaches mean when they say "I pressed the 24s."

The bigger rule behind it matters more than the choice itself: pick one convention, apply it to every dumbbell exercise, and never switch mid-history. A workout log is only useful if today's number can be compared with last month's.

## Why consistency matters more than the convention

Your log has one job: to tell you whether you are getting stronger. That only works if the same number always means the same thing.

Imagine you log dumbbell bench press as 30 kg (per hand) for six weeks, then a training partner tells you to log the total, so you start writing 60 kg. Your progress chart now shows a 100% jump in strength overnight. Any personal best after that point is fake, and any attempt to compare a set from March with a set from May needs mental arithmetic.

The same thing happens in reverse. Switch from total to per hand and it looks like you lost half your strength. If you use a chart to decide when to add weight, as in [double progression](/blog/double-progression), a broken baseline gives you bad decisions.

So when people ask how to log dumbbell weight per hand or total, the real question is not "which is correct?" Both are correct if applied consistently. The question is which convention is **easiest to keep consistent** across every exercise you will ever do. That is where per hand wins.

## How to log dumbbell weight per hand or total: the recommendation

Here is the case for per hand as your default for dumbbell weight tracking.

1. **It is the number you read off the rack.** You pick up the 22.5 kg dumbbells, you type 22.5. No mental doubling between sets when you are tired.
2. **It works for one-arm exercises without a special case.** A one-arm dumbbell row uses one dumbbell. If you log totals, does a single 30 kg dumbbell count as 30 or 60? Per hand removes the question.
3. **It is how people talk.** "I'm benching the 30s" is universal gym language. Your log matches what you say and what programs prescribe.
4. **Programs are written this way.** Most training plans that list a dumbbell load mean the weight of each dumbbell. Logging per hand lets you copy targets straight into your log.
5. **It handles uneven pairs cleanly.** If you are rehabbing a weaker side or the gym has only one 26 kg dumbbell left, per hand lets you log each side's weight separately as needed.

### A worked example

Say you are doing dumbbell shoulder press with a pair of 20 kg (45 lb) dumbbells for 3 sets of 10.

| What you log | Per hand (recommended) | Total |
|---|---|---|
| Weight | 20 kg (45 lb) | 40 kg (90 lb) |
| Reps | 10 | 10 |
| Volume per set | 200 kg (450 lb) per arm | 400 kg (900 lb) |

Either column is fine as long as you never mix them. The per-hand column is just easier to fill in correctly at the rack.

## When logging the total makes sense

There are a few situations where total load is the more natural number.

- **A single dumbbell held with both hands.** Goblet squats, dumbbell pullovers, and two-handed dumbbell Romanian deadlifts with one heavy dumbbell. There is only one dumbbell, so "per hand" and "total" are the same number. Log the dumbbell's weight.
- **You compare against a barbell lift on purpose.** Some lifters like to see dumbbell bench press next to barbell bench press on the same scale. If you do, log totals for that one exercise and name it clearly (more on naming below).
- **Your coach or program prescribes totals.** If someone else reads your log and they think in totals, follow their convention. Consistency with the person programming you matters more than gym convention.

If you go with total for any exercise, make it obvious. A custom exercise called "DB Bench Press (total)" removes any chance of confusion when you look back a year later.

## Unilateral exercises: weight and reps per side

Single-arm and single-leg exercises are where most logs get messy. A clean single-arm exercises log needs two decisions: how to record the weight and how to record the reps.

### Weight on unilateral exercises

Log the weight of the dumbbell in your working hand. For a one-arm dumbbell row with a 32 kg (70 lb) dumbbell, log 32 kg (70 lb). For Bulgarian split squats holding two 16 kg (35 lb) dumbbells, log 16 kg (35 lb), because you are still following the per-hand rule.

### Reps on unilateral exercises

Log **reps per side**, not the total of both sides. If you do 10 reps with the left arm and 10 with the right, log 10, not 20.

Why per side? Because a set of 10 on each arm is the effort you actually did. Logging 20 makes a one-arm row look like a high-rep set and makes it hard to compare with a rep range like 8-12 in your plan.

If your sides do not match, log the weaker side's reps. If your left arm gets 8 and your right gets 10, log 8. The weaker side is the one that limits progress, and it keeps you honest about when to add load.

### Unilateral cheat sheet

| Exercise | Weight to log | Reps to log |
|---|---|---|
| One-arm dumbbell row | Weight of the one dumbbell | Reps per arm |
| Single-arm dumbbell press | Weight of the one dumbbell | Reps per arm |
| Bulgarian split squat (two DBs) | Weight of one dumbbell | Reps per leg |
| Walking lunge (two DBs) | Weight of one dumbbell | Reps per leg (or steps per leg) |
| Alternating dumbbell curl | Weight of one dumbbell | Reps per arm |
| Single-arm cable row | Stack number | Reps per arm |

Alternating curls trip a lot of people up. If you do 10 curls on each arm, alternating, log 10. Your log should describe how many times each arm worked, not how many times a dumbbell moved.

## Cables, machines, and stack units

Cable stacks and selectorized machines raise a different problem: the number on the pin is not always a real weight.

### Log the number you select

For cables and machines, log exactly what the stack says, whether that is kg, lb, or a plate number like "7". You are tracking progress on that machine, and the stack number is the only thing you can reproduce next session.

Don't try to convert stack numbers to "real" kilograms. Pulley ratios, friction, and machine design mean a "50 kg" lat pulldown on one brand can feel very different on another. Converting adds false precision.

### Double cables and pulley ratios

For a cable crossover with two handles, log the stack setting of one side, the same way you log one dumbbell. If both stacks are set to 15 kg, log 15 kg.

Some cable machines have a 2:1 pulley that halves the felt resistance. You do not need to calculate it. Just log the stack number and keep using that same machine for that exercise.

### Plate-loaded machines

For plate-loaded machines like a leg press or a hammer-style chest press, pick one rule and stick to it. The simplest is **plates loaded per side**. If you put two 20 kg plates on each side of a leg press, log 40 kg (or two 45 lb plates per side, 90 lb). Some lifters log total plates (80 kg), which also works. Just do not add the sled weight sometimes and skip it other times.

If you want to log the sled weight too, add it every time and note it in the exercise name, e.g. "Leg Press (incl. sled)". For more on reading plates in kilograms, see [how to load a barbell in kg](/blog/how-to-load-a-barbell-in-kg).

## Bodyweight and assisted exercises

Bodyweight work needs its own rule, because the "weight" is you.

### Pure bodyweight exercises

For push-ups, pull-ups, dips, and similar movements, log **0** (or leave weight blank) and track reps. Do not log your body weight as the load. It changes from week to week, and that change would show up as fake strength gains or losses on your chart.

Your body weight belongs in a separate body-weight log, where it can be charted on its own. If you want to know how bodyweight trends and strength trends relate, look at both charts side by side.

### Weighted bodyweight exercises

For weighted pull-ups or dips, log **only the added weight**. A 10 kg (25 lb) plate on a dip belt is logged as 10 kg (25 lb). If you hold a dumbbell between your feet for pull-ups, log the dumbbell's weight.

### Assisted exercises

Assisted pull-up and dip machines are the tricky one, because more weight on the stack makes the exercise easier. There are two clean options:

1. **Log the assistance as the stack number** and remember that progress means the number going down. Name the exercise "Assisted Pull-up (assistance)" so the downward trend makes sense.
2. **Log reps only** on band-assisted work, and note the band color in the exercise name, e.g. "Pull-up (red band)". When you move to a lighter band, start a new exercise.

The first option is easier to chart. Just be aware that a falling line is good news here, which is the opposite of every other exercise in your log.

## Gym-to-gym weight differences

Dumbbells are not as standardized as you might think. A "25 kg" dumbbell at a commercial gym and a "25 kg" adjustable dumbbell at home can differ, and older fixed dumbbells can wear or be mislabeled.

Practical rules for dumbbell weight tracking across gyms:

- **Log what is stamped on the dumbbell.** Do not try to weigh them or correct the label. Your goal is reproducibility, not lab accuracy.
- **Expect some noise when you switch gyms.** If you move from one gym to another and lose a rep or two for a week, it may be equipment, not you.
- **Treat a permanent gym change as a soft reset.** If you change gyms for good, your first two weeks there set a new baseline. Don't panic about a dip on the chart.
- **Keep machines separate.** A chest press at Gym A and a chest press at Gym B are different exercises. If you train at two gyms regularly, create two custom exercises, e.g. "Chest Press (home gym)" and "Chest Press (office gym)".

## kg vs lb dumbbells and the size of the jumps

Dumbbell increments are one of the most underrated parts of dumbbell weight tracking. The step between dumbbells is often a much bigger percentage jump than a barbell lift allows.

### Typical increments

Most kilogram dumbbell racks go up in **2.5 kg steps** at the lighter end (often 2 kg or 2.5 kg increments, depending on the brand), sometimes moving to bigger steps at the heavy end. Most pound racks go up in **5 lb steps**. Since 2.5 kg is about 5.5 lb, the jumps are similar in size, but the numbers are not interchangeable.

### Why the jump is so big

On a lateral raise, moving from 8 kg to 10 kg (about 17.5 lb to 22.5 lb) is a 25% increase in load. On a barbell squat, you might add 2.5 kg to a 100 kg bar, which is only 2.5%. That is why dumbbell exercises often need more reps before you move up.

| Current dumbbell | Next dumbbell | Jump size |
|---|---|---|
| 8 kg (17.5 lb) | 10 kg (22.5 lb) | +25% |
| 12.5 kg (27.5 lb) | 15 kg (32.5 lb) | +20% |
| 20 kg (45 lb) | 22.5 kg (50 lb) | +12.5% |
| 30 kg (65 lb) | 32.5 kg (70 lb) | about +8% |

Pound values are the nearest standard lb dumbbell, not exact conversions.

The practical fix is to work across a wider rep range before you jump. If your target is 8-12 reps, build up to 12 or even 15 clean reps with the current dumbbell, then move up and accept starting at the bottom of the range. This is exactly the logic behind [double progression](/blog/double-progression).

### Don't mix units in one exercise

If you train in kg, log in kg. If your gym has lb dumbbells, log in lb. Mixing kg and lb inside one exercise history is a guaranteed way to break your charts. In GymLedger you choose kg or lbs as your unit, so pick the one that matches the equipment you use most.

If you travel and use a gym with the other unit, convert once and round to the nearest dumbbell you actually used. Better still, log those sessions under a separate custom exercise so your main history stays clean.

## Naming custom exercises consistently

Most logging mistakes are really naming mistakes. If "DB Bench", "Dumbbell Bench Press", and "Flat DB Press" all exist in your log, your history is split into three short charts instead of one long one.

GymLedger lets you create custom exercises, which is useful precisely because you can encode your logging rule into the name. A simple naming system:

1. **Equipment first or last, never both.** Choose "Dumbbell Bench Press" or "Bench Press (DB)" and use that pattern for everything.
2. **Add the convention only when it differs from your default.** If per hand is your default, don't label it. Label exceptions, e.g. "Goblet Squat" needs no tag, but "DB Bench Press (total)" does.
3. **Mark single-arm work clearly.** "One-Arm DB Row" makes it obvious that reps are per side.
4. **Include the machine or location when it matters.** "Leg Press (plates per side)", "Chest Press (office gym)".
5. **Never rename an exercise to mean something different.** If you change convention, create a new exercise rather than redefining the old one.

A short naming system like this takes five minutes to set up and saves you from ever wondering what a number in your log meant.

## Keeping your progress charts accurate

A per-exercise strength chart is only as good as the data in it. Here is how to keep it clean.

- **Log working sets the same way every time.** If you sometimes log warm-up sets and sometimes don't, your session totals will jump around. Pick a rule, such as logging only working sets, and stick with it.
- **Use the same exercise entry every session.** Don't create a new "Incline DB Press 2" because you could not find the original. Search for it.
- **Change exercises, not definitions.** If you switch from flat to a slight incline, that's a new exercise with its own chart.
- **Watch for sudden spikes.** A one-session doubling of weight on a dumbbell exercise almost always means a per-hand/total mix-up. Fix it straight away while you remember.

GymLedger shows per-exercise strength progress charts and personal bests, which makes this easy to spot: a jump that looks too good to be true on a dumbbell exercise usually is a logging error. For the bigger picture on tracking progress, see [how to track progressive overload](/blog/how-to-track-progressive-overload).

## How to fix old, inconsistent logs

If your history is already a mix of per hand and total, you have a few options. None of them require throwing the data away.

### Option 1: Draw a line and start fresh

The fastest fix. Decide on per hand from today, create new custom exercises with clear names, and use those going forward. Your old data stays as a rough reference. Within four to six weeks you will have a clean baseline that matters more than old numbers anyway.

### Option 2: Correct the recent entries

If the mix-up is limited to a few weeks and your app lets you edit past sessions, go back and fix those sets. Look for numbers that are roughly double (or half) their neighbors. A dumbbell bench history reading 28, 28, 30, 60, 60, 30 kg makes the two bad entries obvious: they are totals, and the correct per-hand number is 30 kg (65 lb).

### Option 3: Keep both, labeled

If you genuinely used both conventions for long stretches, rename the old exercise to something like "DB Bench Press (old, mixed)" and start a fresh one. You keep the history for reference without polluting the chart you use for decisions.

Whichever you choose, the important part is that from today on, every dumbbell entry follows the same rule.

## Putting it into practice

Here is the full rule set in one place, so you can apply it to every way you log dumbbell exercises:

1. **Dumbbells:** log the weight of one dumbbell (per hand).
2. **Single dumbbell, two hands:** log that dumbbell's weight.
3. **Unilateral exercises:** log the dumbbell in the working hand and reps per side; if sides differ, log the weaker side.
4. **Cables and machines:** log the stack number exactly as shown; for double cables, one side.
5. **Plate-loaded machines:** log plates per side (or total), but always the same way.
6. **Bodyweight:** log 0 or blank; for weighted versions, log only the added load.
7. **Assisted machines:** log the assistance and remember lower is better.
8. **Units:** one unit per exercise history, matching your gym's equipment.
9. **Names:** label exceptions in the exercise name, never redefine an existing exercise.

Set up your custom exercises once with these rules baked into the names, and your log will stay readable for years. If you are still building your routine, [build your own workout split](/blog/how-to-build-your-own-workout-split) first, then set up the exercise names to match.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do you count both dumbbells when logging weight?

Most lifters do not. The common convention is to log the weight of one dumbbell, so a pair of 20 kg (45 lb) dumbbells is logged as 20 kg (45 lb). It matches the number on the dumbbell and how programs are written. Counting both is not wrong, but you must do it for every entry of that exercise, or your progress chart will show fake jumps.

### How do you log reps for single-arm exercises?

Log reps per side, not the total of both sides. If you do 10 one-arm rows on each side, log 10. If your sides do not match, log the reps of the weaker side, because it is the side limiting your progress. This keeps single-arm exercises comparable with the rep ranges in your plan and makes it clear when you have earned a heavier dumbbell.

### Should I include my body weight when logging pull-ups or dips?

No. Log zero or leave the weight blank for bodyweight sets, and track reps. For weighted pull-ups or dips, log only the added weight, such as a 10 kg (25 lb) plate on a belt. Your body weight changes over time, so including it would make your strength chart move for reasons that have nothing to do with the exercise.

### How do I log the weight on a cable machine?

Log the number on the stack exactly as shown, whether it is kilograms, pounds, or a plain plate number. Do not try to convert it to a true weight, because pulley ratios and machine design vary between brands. For two-handle cable exercises, log one side's stack setting. Keep using the same machine for that exercise so the numbers stay comparable.

### Why do dumbbell jumps feel so much harder than barbell jumps?

Because the percentage increase is much larger. Moving from an 8 kg to a 10 kg dumbbell (about 17.5 lb to 22.5 lb) is a 25% jump, while adding 2.5 kg to a 100 kg barbell is only 2.5%. Build up more reps with your current dumbbell before moving up, and expect to start the heavier weight at the bottom of your rep range.

### What should I do if my old dumbbell logs mix per hand and total?

Either correct the recent entries that look roughly double or half their neighbors, or draw a line and start fresh with clearly named custom exercises. If you used both conventions for a long time, rename the old exercise to mark it as mixed and keep it for reference. What matters is that every entry from today follows one rule.
