What is a deload week in lifting? It's a planned week of easier training, usually about half your normal sets or 10 to 20% less weight on the bar, that lets built-up fatigue clear so you can keep making progress. You still go to the gym and do the same lifts, just with less work. Most lifters who train hard take one every 4 to 8 weeks, or when their log shows several lifts stuck for several sessions in a row.

A deload is recovery on the scale of weeks. If you want the day-to-day version first, how long each muscle needs between sessions, see our guide to [how long it takes for muscles to recover](/blog/how-long-does-it-take-for-muscles-to-recover).

## What is a deload week in lifting?

Hard training does two things at once: it builds strength and muscle, and it piles up fatigue. Over a few weeks the fatigue can grow faster than you clear it. Your lifts stall, your joints start complaining, and every session feels heavier than the plates say it should. A deload drops the training stress for a week so the fatigue clears while the strength and muscle stay.

In a 2023 consensus study, a panel of strength and physique coaches agreed on a definition: a deload is a period of reduced training stress meant to lower physical and mental fatigue, help recovery, and leave you better prepared for the training that follows ([Bell et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37730925/)).

A deload is not the same as a week off:

| | Deload week | Rest week | Normal week |
|---|---|---|---|
| Do you lift? | Yes, same days and same exercises | No, or almost none | Yes |
| Sets | About half | None | Full |
| Weight on the bar | Same, or 10 to 20% lighter | None | Full |
| Effort | 3 to 4 reps short of failure | None | Hard |
| When to use it | Planned every few weeks, or when fatigue builds up | Illness, injury, travel, burnout | Most of the time |

### How experienced lifters deload

A 2024 survey asked 246 competitive strength and physique athletes how they deload ([Rogerson et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38499934/)). Their answers make a sensible template:

- A deload lasted about **6 to 7 days** on average, roughly every **5 to 6 weeks**.
- They cut volume, with fewer sets per week and fewer reps per set.
- They lowered the weight and stopped further from failure.
- They kept the **same exercises** and the **same number of training days**.
- They deloaded on a planned schedule, when performance stalled, or when soreness and joint aches built up.

## Why am I not getting stronger? Signs you need a deload

Not every stall means you need a deload. A single lift stuck for a week or two is usually a programming problem with that lift: the jumps are too big, the rep target is too ambitious, or you've been grinding the same weight for too long. A deload helps when **several lifts stall at once** and other signs of fatigue show up alongside.

| Sign | What it looks like in practice | Deload? |
|---|---|---|
| Stalled lifts across several sessions | Bench, squat and row all stuck or dropping for 2 to 3 sessions each | Yes, the strongest sign |
| Joints achy | Elbows sore on pressing, knees grumbling on squats, and it isn't going away | Yes, especially alongside stalls |
| Sleep off | Waking up tired, or restless sleep after training days | Maybe; fix sleep habits too |
| Soreness that never clears | Every session starts sore from the last one | Yes, if your lifts are also stalling |
| Sessions feel heavy before they start | Warm-up weights feel like working weights | Maybe; one bad day is noise |
| One lift stuck, everything else moving | Overhead press stuck at 50 kg (110 lb) for 3 weeks | No; change how you progress that lift |

### Reading the log: a worked example

Here's what a deload-worthy log looks like. Three lifts, last four sessions of each:

| Session | Bench press | Squat | Barbell row |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 80 kg (175 lb) × 8, 8, 7 | 100 kg (220 lb) × 6, 6, 6 | 60 kg (130 lb) × 10, 10, 9 |
| 2 | 80 kg × 8, 7, 7 | 102.5 kg (225 lb) × 6, 5, 5 | 62.5 kg (140 lb) × 9, 9, 8 |
| 3 | 80 kg × 7, 7, 6 | 102.5 kg × 5, 5, 4 | 62.5 kg × 9, 8, 8 |
| 4 | 80 kg × 7, 6, 6 | 102.5 kg × 5, 4, 4 | 62.5 kg × 8, 8, 7 |

All three lifts stalled at the same time and are now sliding backwards at unchanged weights. That pattern points to fatigue rather than a problem with one lift's program. If you'd also been sleeping badly and your elbows ached, this would be an easy call.

### When a deload won't fix it

A deload clears fatigue. It doesn't fix:

- **Eating too little.** On a hard cut, strength often stalls no matter how you program.
- **Chronic short sleep.** A week of light training won't cancel months of five-hour nights.
- **A program with no progression built in.** If you've done 3 × 10 at 60 kg (130 lb) for six months, you need a plan for adding weight or reps.

If you deload and the stall comes straight back within a week or two, look at those three first.

## Planned vs reactive deloads, and how often to take one

There are two ways to schedule a deload, and most experienced lifters use a mix.

**Planned deloads** go in the calendar ahead of time, for example every fifth or sixth week. They're simple, and they stop you from talking yourself out of one when you "feel fine".

**Reactive deloads** happen when the signs above show up. They match your actual recovery, but they rely on you noticing the signs and being honest about them.

How often depends mostly on how hard and how long you've been training:

| Lifter | Suggested deload frequency |
|---|---|
| Beginner (first 6 to 12 months) | Rarely needed; deload reactively if several lifts stall together |
| Intermediate, normal volume | Every 6 to 8 weeks, or reactively |
| Advanced, high volume, or training 5 to 6 days a week | Every 4 to 6 weeks |
| Dieting hard, over 40, or under heavy life stress | Toward the shorter end, or reactively when signs appear |

The survey's average of a deload roughly every 5 to 6 weeks comes from competitive athletes training hard, so treat it as the upper end of frequency for most recreational lifters.

### Why a deload beats a week off for strength

You might wonder whether to skip the gym entirely instead. One study tested that: trained lifters on a 9-week program either trained straight through or took a complete week off in the middle. Muscle growth was similar, but the group that trained straight through gained more lower-body strength ([Coleman et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/)).

That study used a full week off, not a lighter week, but it's a useful hint. If strength is the goal, keep lifting during your deload and keep some weight on the bar. Save full weeks off for illness, injury or holidays.

## How to deload: four methods with worked examples

Pick one method for the whole week. In all four, keep the same exercises and the same training days, stay 3 to 4 reps short of failure on every set, and don't test a max.

| Method | What changes | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Cut sets about in half | Volume down, weight the same | Strength-focused lifters who want to keep the feel of heavy weights |
| 2. Cut weight 10 to 20% | Weight down, sets the same | Achy joints, or higher-rep hypertrophy programs |
| 3. Cut both | Sets about half, weight down 10 to 20% | Really beaten up, at the end of a long or hard block, or during a cut |
| 4. Active rest | Little or no lifting; walking, cycling, light circuits | Travel, minor injuries, mental burnout |

### Method 1: cut sets about in half, keep the weight

**Normal week:** squat 4 × 6 at **100 kg (220 lb)**, bench press 4 × 8 at **80 kg (175 lb)**
**Deload week:** squat 2 × 6 at **100 kg (220 lb)**, bench press 2 × 8 at **80 kg (175 lb)**

Stop each set where it feels easy. If your normal first set of 6 squats left 2 reps in the tank, this week's sets should feel like they leave 3 or 4. If a set starts to grind, end it there.

### Method 2: cut the weight 10 to 20%, keep the sets

**Normal week:** squat 4 × 6 at **100 kg (220 lb)**, bench press 4 × 8 at **80 kg (175 lb)**
**Deload week:** squat 4 × 6 at **85 kg (185 lb)**, bench press 4 × 8 at **70 kg (155 lb)**

That's 15% off the squat and about 12% off the bench, rounded to weights you can load. Use the lower end (10%) if you only feel a bit flat, and the higher end (20%) if your joints are sore.

### Method 3: cut both sets and weight

**Deload week:** squat 2 × 6 at **85 kg (185 lb)**, bench press 2 × 8 at **70 kg (155 lb)**

This is the biggest reduction short of not lifting. Sessions will take about half the usual time, which is fine. Resist the urge to fill the gap with an extra hour of cardio.

### Method 4: active rest

Skip the barbell for the week. Walk every day, cycle or swim easily two or three times, and if you want to lift, do one or two light full-body circuits: goblet squats, push-ups and dumbbell rows, 2 sets of 10 each, nowhere near failure.

Use this one when you need a break from the gym itself. Given the study above, don't make it your default deload if strength is your main goal.

If you want a clearer sense of "3 to 4 reps short of failure", our [RPE chart](/blog/rpe-chart) explains how to rate effort. A deload set should feel like RPE 6 or 7 at most.

## Deload workouts: sample weeks for PPL and upper/lower

Here's what a deload week looks like on the two most common splits.

### Push/pull/legs (6 days), method 1

Same days, same exercises, half the sets:

| Day | Normal session | Deload session |
|---|---|---|
| Push | Bench 4 × 6 at 80 kg (175 lb), overhead press 3 × 8 at 45 kg (100 lb), incline dumbbell press 3 × 10, lateral raise 3 × 15, pushdown 3 × 12 | Bench 2 × 6 at 80 kg (175 lb), overhead press 2 × 8 at 45 kg (100 lb), incline dumbbell press 2 × 10, lateral raise 2 × 15, pushdown 2 × 12 |
| Pull | Barbell row 4 × 8 at 60 kg (130 lb), pull-down 3 × 10, cable row 3 × 12, curl 3 × 12 | Barbell row 2 × 8 at 60 kg (130 lb), pull-down 2 × 10, cable row 2 × 12, curl 2 × 12 |
| Legs | Squat 4 × 6 at 100 kg (220 lb), Romanian deadlift 3 × 8 at 80 kg (175 lb), leg press 3 × 12, calf raise 3 × 15 | Squat 2 × 6 at 100 kg (220 lb), Romanian deadlift 2 × 8 at 70 kg (155 lb), leg press 2 × 12, calf raise 2 × 15 |

The second round of push, pull and legs that week repeats the same deload sessions. Notice the RDL also drops weight: lowering-heavy lifts beat up the hamstrings and lower back more than their set count suggests. ### Upper/lower (4 days), method 2

Same sets, about 15% less weight:

| Day | Normal session | Deload session |
|---|---|---|
| Upper A | Bench 4 × 5 at 85 kg (185 lb), row 4 × 8 at 60 kg (130 lb), overhead press 3 × 8 at 45 kg (100 lb) | Bench 4 × 5 at 72.5 kg (160 lb), row 4 × 8 at 50 kg (110 lb), overhead press 3 × 8 at 37.5 kg (85 lb) |
| Lower A | Squat 4 × 5 at 110 kg (245 lb), Romanian deadlift 3 × 8 at 90 kg (200 lb), leg curl 3 × 12 | Squat 4 × 5 at 92.5 kg (205 lb), Romanian deadlift 3 × 8 at 75 kg (165 lb), leg curl 3 × 12, lighter |
| Upper B | Incline press, pull-ups, dips, curls at your usual weights | Same exercises, about 15% lighter, or bodyweight pull-ups and dips stopped 4 reps short |
| Lower B | Deadlift 3 × 5 at 140 kg (310 lb), leg press 3 × 10, calf raise 3 × 15 | Deadlift 3 × 5 at 120 kg (265 lb), leg press 3 × 10 lighter, calf raise 3 × 15 |

## Coming back after a deload

The first week back decides whether the deload worked.

**Go back to your pre-deload weights.** Use the weights from your last good session, not your best-ever numbers with extra on top. Those weights often move better than they did before the deload.

**Worked example.** Before the deload, your bench was stuck at 80 kg (175 lb) for 7, 6, 6. In the first session back:

- **If it moves well:** 80 kg (175 lb) for 8, 7, 7. You're progressing again; carry on with your usual method, such as [double progression](/blog/double-progression).
- **If it's still stuck:** reset to about 90% and build back with smaller jumps: 72.5 kg (160 lb) for 3 × 8, then 75 kg, 77.5 kg and 80 kg over the next four sessions, adding reps before weight.

Expect a little soreness in the first session or two back, because volume jumps back up. It fades quickly. If it hits harder than that, our guide to [sore muscles after a workout](/blog/sore-muscles-after-workout) covers when to train through it.

## Common deload mistakes

- **Turning it into a test week.** "I'm fresh, so let's max" undoes the point. Test after the deload if you want to test.
- **Filling the spare time with something harder.** A deload week of light lifting plus three hard spin classes isn't a deload.
- **Skipping it because you feel fine.** If your log shows three lifts going backwards, believe the log.
- **Deloading too often.** If you deload every three weeks and nothing has stalled, you're giving up training time for no reason.
- **Changing all the exercises.** Keep the same lifts so the week after picks up cleanly.

## Putting it into practice

1. A deload is a planned lighter week: same days, same exercises, about half the sets or 10 to 20% less weight, every set 3 to 4 reps short of failure.
2. Take one when several lifts stall together for two or three sessions, especially alongside achy joints, poor sleep or soreness that never clears.
3. Plan one every 6 to 8 weeks as an intermediate lifter, every 4 to 6 if you train hard and often, and only reactively as a beginner.
4. Pick one method for the week. Cutting sets suits strength work; cutting weight suits achy joints; cutting both suits the end of a hard block.
5. Keep lifting during the week. Save full weeks off for illness, injury and holidays.
6. Come back at your pre-deload weights, and reset by about 10% if the stall returns.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a deload week in lifting?

A deload week is a planned week of lighter training, usually about half your normal sets or 10 to 20% less weight, with every set stopped well short of failure. You keep training on the same days with the same exercises. The point is to let fatigue from weeks of hard training clear so your lifts start moving again, without losing the strength and muscle you've built.

### How often should you take a deload week?

Most intermediate lifters do well with a deload every 6 to 8 weeks, and lifters training hard five or six days a week often need one every 4 to 6 weeks. Beginners rarely need planned deloads. Whatever your level, take one sooner if several lifts stall together for two or three sessions and you're also seeing achy joints or poor sleep.

### Will I lose muscle during a deload week?

No. One week of lighter training isn't long enough to lose meaningful muscle, especially when you keep lifting with the same exercises. Even a full week off in the middle of a nine-week program didn't reduce muscle growth in one study, though strength gains were a little smaller. Keeping some weight on the bar during the deload protects your strength too.

### Should I deload or take a full week off?

For most lifters a deload is the better choice. You keep practising your lifts, you keep the habit of going to the gym, and research suggests strength holds up better than with a complete break. Save full weeks off for illness, injury, travel or real burnout, and come back from them more gradually than you would from a deload.

### Do beginners need deload weeks?

Usually not on a schedule. In your first six to twelve months, you're lifting light enough loads that fatigue rarely builds up faster than you recover, and missed sessions or holidays act as natural breaks. Take a deload when several lifts stall at once for two or three sessions. If only one lift is stuck, change how you progress that lift instead.
