Here's how to create your own workout split: decide how many days you can realistically train, aim to hit each muscle group about **twice a week**, give each muscle roughly **10-20 hard sets per week**, then choose exercises, order them big-to-small and assign rep ranges. Run the plan for 4-8 weeks, review your logs, and adjust. That's the whole process, and the steps below walk through each part with templates for 3, 4, 5 and 6 days a week.

A premade program is a fine start, but it rarely fits your schedule, equipment and weak points perfectly. Building your own lets you keep the parts that matter and drop the parts that don't.

## Step 1: Decide how many days you can actually train

Start with your calendar, not your ambition. The best split is the one you can repeat for months, not the one that looks impressive for two weeks.

Ask yourself:

- How many days per week can I train **every** week, including busy ones?
- How long is each session? 45 minutes and 90 minutes lead to very different plans.
- Are my training days fixed (Mon/Wed/Fri) or do they move around?

If you're unsure, pick the lower number. Three sessions you never miss beat five sessions you hit half the time. If you later find extra time, you can add a day.

| Days per week | Good split options |
|---|---|
| 2 | Full body x2 |
| 3 | Full body x3, or upper/lower/full |
| 4 | Upper/lower x2 |
| 5 | Upper/lower + push/pull/legs hybrid |
| 6 | Push/pull/legs x2 |

If your days tend to shift, splits that don't rely on fixed weekdays (rotating through workouts in order) are more forgiving. Our guide on [what to do when you miss a day on push/pull/legs](/blog/missed-a-day-push-pull-legs) covers that in detail.

## Step 2: Choose how often to train each muscle

Training frequency is how many times per week each muscle gets worked. For most people building muscle, **twice per week** is the sweet spot.

A 2016 meta-analysis by Schoenfeld and colleagues found that, when weekly volume was equal, training a muscle group twice per week produced better hypertrophy than once per week ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27102172/)). The old "bro split" (chest Monday, back Tuesday, and so on) hits each muscle once, which is why most modern programs have moved away from it.

Why does frequency help? Partly because it spreads your weekly sets out. Twenty sets for chest in one session means the last ten are done tired and sloppy. Split into two sessions of ten, every set gets better effort.

Practical guidelines:

- **Beginners:** 2-3x per week per muscle via full-body sessions works very well.
- **Intermediates:** 2x per week via upper/lower or push/pull/legs twice.
- **Lagging muscles:** can go to 3x per week if recovery allows.

## Step 3: Set weekly sets per muscle

Weekly volume, the number of hard sets per muscle per week, is one of the biggest levers for muscle growth. A 2017 meta-analysis by Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger found a graded dose-response relationship: more weekly sets were associated with greater muscle gain ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/)).

More isn't endlessly better, though. Recovery, time and joint health set a ceiling. A sensible framework:

| Training level | Hard sets per muscle per week |
|---|---|
| Beginner (under 1 year) | 8-12 |
| Intermediate (1-3 years) | 10-16 |
| Advanced (3+ years) | 14-20+ |

A "hard set" is a working set taken reasonably close to failure (around 1-3 reps left). Warm-ups don't count.

Count sets for each major muscle group: chest, back, shoulders (side and rear delts), quads, hamstrings, glutes, biceps, triceps, calves and abs. Compound lifts count toward more than one muscle. A bench press set counts for chest and partly for triceps and front delts, so you need fewer direct triceps sets than you might think.

**Example:** you want 12 chest sets per week across two sessions. That could be 4 sets of bench press plus 2 sets of cable fly on day one, and 3 sets of incline dumbbell press plus 3 sets of machine press on day two.

## Step 4: Pick your exercises

For each muscle, choose one or two exercises per session. Good exercise selection follows a few rules:

1. **Start with a compound lift** for each major area: a squat or leg press pattern, a hinge (deadlift or Romanian deadlift), a horizontal press, a vertical press, a horizontal pull and a vertical pull.
2. **Add isolation work** for muscles compounds don't fully cover: side delts, biceps, triceps, hamstrings (knee flexion), calves.
3. **Choose exercises you can load progressively.** You want movements where you can add weight or reps over weeks and track it cleanly.
4. **Respect your body and gym.** If barbell back squats hurt your hips, a hack squat or leg press is a perfectly good substitute. If your gym has three benches and fifty people, have a dumbbell alternative ready.
5. **Keep it stable.** Rotating exercises every week makes progress hard to measure. Stick with the same main lifts for at least one 4-8 week block.

### Movement patterns cheat sheet

| Pattern | Barbell option | Dumbbell / machine option |
|---|---|---|
| Squat | Back squat | Leg press, goblet squat, hack squat |
| Hinge | Romanian deadlift | Dumbbell RDL, 45-degree back extension |
| Horizontal push | Bench press | Dumbbell press, machine chest press |
| Vertical push | Overhead press | Seated dumbbell press, machine shoulder press |
| Horizontal pull | Barbell row | Chest-supported row, cable row |
| Vertical pull | Pull-up / chin-up | Lat pulldown |
| Single leg | Barbell lunge | Bulgarian split squat, step-up |

## Step 5: Order exercises in each session

Exercise order affects how much you can lift. Put the things that matter most first, while you're fresh.

1. **Big compound lifts first.** Squats, deadlifts, presses and rows need the most energy and coordination.
2. **Secondary compounds next.** Lunges, dumbbell presses, pulldowns.
3. **Isolation work last.** Curls, raises, extensions, calf work.
4. **Priority overrides the rule.** If your side delts lag, you can put lateral raises first on one day.

Rest periods follow the same logic: longer rests for the heavy lifts at the start, shorter rests for isolation work at the end. Our guide on [rest between sets for compound vs isolation exercises](/blog/rest-between-sets-compound-vs-isolation) has a full table of rest times.

## Step 6: Assign rep ranges

Muscle grows across a wide range of reps as long as sets are hard. Strength is more specific: heavier, lower-rep work builds it best. A practical split:

| Exercise type | Rep range | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Main compound (strength focus) | 4-6 | Heavy load, builds strength |
| Main compound (muscle focus) | 6-10 | Good balance of load and volume |
| Secondary compound | 8-12 | Moderate load, more reps |
| Isolation | 10-15 | Joint-friendly, easy to push close to failure |
| Calves, abs, raises | 12-20 | Small muscles, lighter loads |

Using rep **ranges** rather than fixed numbers makes progression simple. With [double progression](/blog/double-progression), you work at a weight until you hit the top of the range on all sets, then add weight and drop back to the bottom.

**Worked example:** your Romanian deadlift range is 8-10 reps.

- Week 1: 80 kg (175 lb) for 8, 8, 8.
- Week 3: 80 kg (175 lb) for 10, 10, 9.
- Week 4: 80 kg (175 lb) for 10, 10, 10. Target hit.
- Week 5: 85 kg (185 lb) for 8, 8, 8. Back to the bottom of the range with more load.

## Step 7: Put it into a weekly template

Now combine everything into actual days. Below are templates for 3, 4, 5 and 6 days. Each hits every major muscle about twice per week (the 3-day full-body plan hits most three times). Adjust sets to match your level from Step 3.

### 3-day full body template

Best for beginners or anyone with limited time. Train on non-consecutive days, like Mon/Wed/Fri.

| Day A | Day B | Day C |
|---|---|---|
| Back squat 3 x 6-8 | Romanian deadlift 3 x 8-10 | Leg press 3 x 10-12 |
| Bench press 3 x 6-8 | Overhead press 3 x 6-8 | Incline dumbbell press 3 x 8-10 |
| Barbell row 3 x 8-10 | Lat pulldown 3 x 8-10 | Cable row 3 x 10-12 |
| Leg curl 2 x 10-12 | Walking lunge 2 x 10/leg | Leg curl 2 x 10-12 |
| Lateral raise 2 x 12-15 | Dumbbell curl 2 x 10-12 | Triceps pushdown 2 x 10-12 |

### 4-day upper/lower template

The most popular intermediate setup. A common schedule is Mon/Tue/Thu/Fri.

| Upper 1 | Lower 1 | Upper 2 | Lower 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bench press 4 x 5-7 | Back squat 4 x 5-7 | Overhead press 3 x 6-8 | Romanian deadlift 3 x 6-8 |
| Barbell row 4 x 6-8 | Romanian deadlift 3 x 8-10 | Pull-up or pulldown 3 x 8-10 | Leg press 3 x 10-12 |
| Seated dumbbell press 3 x 8-10 | Leg extension 3 x 12-15 | Incline dumbbell press 3 x 8-10 | Bulgarian split squat 3 x 8-10/leg |
| Lat pulldown 3 x 10-12 | Leg curl 3 x 10-12 | Chest-supported row 3 x 10-12 | Leg curl 3 x 10-12 |
| Dumbbell curl 3 x 10-12 | Calf raise 3 x 12-15 | Lateral raise 3 x 12-15 | Calf raise 3 x 12-15 |
| Triceps pushdown 3 x 10-12 | | Overhead triceps extension 2 x 10-12 | |

### 5-day upper/lower + push/pull/legs template

Combines the two styles. Upper and lower early in the week, then push, pull and legs. A schedule could be Mon/Tue/Thu/Fri/Sat.

| Upper | Lower | Push | Pull | Legs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bench press 3 x 5-7 | Back squat 3 x 5-7 | Incline dumbbell press 3 x 8-10 | Pull-up or pulldown 3 x 8-10 | Leg press 3 x 10-12 |
| Barbell row 3 x 6-8 | Romanian deadlift 3 x 8-10 | Seated dumbbell press 3 x 8-10 | Chest-supported row 3 x 10-12 | Romanian deadlift 3 x 8-10 |
| Overhead press 3 x 6-8 | Leg curl 3 x 10-12 | Cable fly 3 x 12-15 | Face pull 3 x 12-15 | Leg extension 3 x 12-15 |
| Lat pulldown 3 x 8-10 | Calf raise 3 x 12-15 | Lateral raise 3 x 12-15 | Dumbbell curl 3 x 10-12 | Leg curl 3 x 10-12 |
| | | Triceps pushdown 3 x 10-12 | Hammer curl 2 x 10-12 | Calf raise 3 x 12-15 |

### 6-day push/pull/legs template

For experienced lifters with time and good recovery. Run push, pull, legs, then repeat, with one rest day.

| Push (x2) | Pull (x2) | Legs (x2) |
|---|---|---|
| Bench press 3 x 6-8 | Barbell or cable row 3 x 8-10 | Back squat or hack squat 3 x 6-8 |
| Overhead press 3 x 8-10 | Pull-up or pulldown 3 x 8-10 | Romanian deadlift 3 x 8-10 |
| Incline dumbbell press 2 x 10-12 | Chest-supported row 2 x 10-12 | Leg press or lunge 2 x 10-12 |
| Lateral raise 3 x 12-15 | Face pull 3 x 12-15 | Leg curl 3 x 10-12 |
| Triceps pushdown 3 x 10-12 | Dumbbell curl 3 x 10-12 | Calf raise 3 x 12-15 |

On the second round of the week, you can swap in variations (for example, incline bench instead of flat), but keep the same variations week to week so progress stays measurable.

### Sanity-check your weekly volume

Once your template is drafted, count hard sets per muscle. Take chest in the 4-day upper/lower plan:

- Upper 1: bench press 4 + seated dumbbell press (partial) = about 4-5 sets.
- Upper 2: incline dumbbell press 3 = 3 sets.

That's roughly 7-8 direct sets, which is fine for a beginner but light for an intermediate. You could add 2 sets of cable fly to each upper day to land around 11-12. Repeat this check for every muscle and you'll catch gaps before they become weak points.

## How to test and adjust your split after 4-8 weeks

A split isn't finished when you write it. It's finished when your logs say it works. Run the plan unchanged for at least four weeks, ideally six to eight, then review.

### What to look for

1. **Are your main lifts going up?** Compare the weight and reps on your key exercises from week 1 to now. If your bench went from 60 kg (135 lb) x 6 to 65 kg (145 lb) x 7, that's real progress.
2. **Which exercises stalled?** If one lift hasn't moved in three or more weeks, it may need more rest, a different rep range, or a swap.
3. **Did you complete the sessions?** If you keep skipping the last two exercises, your sessions are too long. Cut volume or merge exercises.
4. **How do you feel?** Persistent joint aches, poor sleep or dreading the gym can mean too much volume. Constant freshness with no progress can mean too little.
5. **Body weight trend.** If you're trying to build muscle and your weight isn't moving, food may be the bottleneck rather than the split. Our guide on [how often to weigh yourself](/blog/how-often-to-weigh-yourself) explains how to read that trend.

### Adjustments to make

| What you see | What to change |
|---|---|
| Everything progressing, feel good | Change nothing. Run another block. |
| A muscle lagging, recovering well | Add 2-4 weekly sets for that muscle |
| Joints sore, performance dropping | Remove 2-4 sets per muscle or add a rest day |
| Sessions running too long | Superset accessories or drop a low-value exercise |
| One lift stuck for 3+ weeks | Swap the variation or change the rep range |
| Missing sessions often | Move to a split with fewer days |

Change **one or two things at a time**. If you change everything at once, you won't know what worked.

## How to create your own workout split: quick checklist

Here's the condensed version you can act on today:

1. **Count your realistic training days** and pick a split from the Step 1 table.
2. **Hit each muscle about twice a week.**
3. **Start at the low end of the weekly sets range** for your level.
4. **Choose one compound per movement pattern**, then fill gaps with isolation work.
5. **Order big lifts first**, isolation last.
6. **Assign rep ranges** and use double progression.
7. **Run it 4-8 weeks unchanged**, then review your logs and adjust one or two things.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best workout split for building muscle?

There's no single best split. Research suggests training each muscle about twice per week works better than once, so upper/lower, full-body and push/pull/legs run twice a week are all strong choices. The best one is whichever fits the number of days you can consistently train. Pick the split you can follow for months, then focus on adding weight or reps over time.

### How many exercises should be in a workout?

Most effective sessions have 4-6 exercises. That's usually one or two compound lifts followed by two to four accessory or isolation exercises. Fewer exercises with more quality sets tends to beat a long list of exercises done for one or two sets each. If your sessions regularly run longer than 75-90 minutes or you're skipping the final exercises, trim the list.

### How many sets per muscle per week should I do?

A good starting range is 10-20 hard sets per muscle per week, with beginners toward 8-12 and advanced lifters toward the top. Research shows more weekly sets are associated with more muscle growth, but recovery limits how far you can push it. Start lower, track your progress, and add a few sets to muscles that lag while recovery stays good.

### How long should I follow a workout split before changing it?

Run a new split for at least 4 weeks, and ideally 6-8, before making changes. That gives you enough sessions to see whether your main lifts are progressing and whether recovery is manageable. Changing programs every week or two makes progress impossible to measure. When you do adjust, change one or two variables at a time so you can tell what made the difference.

### Can I design a workout plan with only 3 days a week?

Yes. Three full-body sessions per week let you train each muscle three times, which is plenty for beginners and works well for intermediates too. Put compound lifts first, add a few isolation exercises, and keep sessions around 45-75 minutes. Many people make excellent progress on three days because it's easy to stay consistent and recover between sessions.
