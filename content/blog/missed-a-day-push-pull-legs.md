If you missed a day on push pull legs, the simplest fix is to **do the session you missed next time you train** and let everything shift forward by one day. Don't skip the workout, don't double up, and don't restart the week. One missed day changes almost nothing about your progress; how you respond to it is what matters.

The rest of this guide covers the details: the difference between a rolling and a fixed-calendar PPL, clear decision rules for one missed day, several missed days, and a week or more off, when merging sessions makes sense, and how to bring your weights back after a break.

## Missed a day on push pull legs? Why it's not a big deal

Push/pull/legs (PPL) splits your training into three sessions: **push** (chest, shoulders, triceps), **pull** (back, biceps, rear delts), and **legs** (quads, hamstrings, glutes, calves). Most people run it either three days a week (each session once) or six days a week (each session twice).

Strength and muscle are built over months, not individual workouts. If you train 4 to 6 times a week for a year, that's 200 to 300 sessions. Losing one of them is well under 1% of your training. The real risk of a missed day is not lost muscle; it's the knock-on effect: skipping legs because the calendar says it's Thursday, or quitting for the week because the "plan is ruined."

So the goal when you miss a session is simple: **keep the order of your sessions intact and keep the habit going.**

## Rolling vs fixed calendar: two ways to run PPL

Before you decide what to do after you've missed a day on push pull legs, you need to know which kind of schedule you're running. This is the core of the rotating split vs weekly schedule question.

### The fixed-calendar (weekly) schedule

A fixed schedule ties each workout to a weekday:

| Day | 6-day PPL | 3-day PPL |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Push | Push |
| Tuesday | Pull | Rest |
| Wednesday | Legs | Pull |
| Thursday | Push | Rest |
| Friday | Pull | Legs |
| Saturday | Legs | Rest |
| Sunday | Rest | Rest |

**Pros:** easy to plan around work and family, and you always know what Monday looks like.
**Cons:** when life gets in the way, the calendar keeps moving without you. Miss Wednesday and the plan says Thursday is push, so legs quietly disappears from your week.

### The rolling (rotating) schedule

A rolling schedule ignores weekdays and simply cycles through the sessions in order: push, pull, legs, rest, push, pull, legs, rest, and so on. The next workout is always "whatever comes after the last one I did."

**Pros:** a missed day never deletes a session. Everything just shifts forward by one day. Every muscle group gets the same amount of work over time.
**Cons:** your training days drift across the week, so Monday might be legs one week and pull the next. That can clash with a fixed rest day or a busy schedule.

### Which one should you use?

If your weeks are predictable, a fixed calendar is fine, as long as you have a rule for missed days (covered below). If your schedule is irregular (shift work, travel, college exams, small kids), a rolling schedule is usually the better fit because it absorbs disruptions automatically.

Many lifters end up with a hybrid: **a rolling order with a fixed rest day.** You cycle push, pull, legs in sequence, but Sunday is always off no matter where you are in the rotation. That gives you the flexibility of rolling with one predictable anchor in the week.

If you're still choosing a split, our guide on [how to build your own workout split](/blog/how-to-build-your-own-workout-split) walks through the trade-offs between PPL, upper/lower, and full body.

## Decision rules: what to do if you miss a workout

Here is the short version. Find your situation and follow the rule.

| Situation | What to do | Weights when you return |
|---|---|---|
| Missed 1 day | Do the missed session next; shift everything forward | Same as last time; keep progressing |
| Missed 2 to 4 days | Resume with the session you missed; don't try to catch up | Same as last time, or 5% lighter if you feel flat |
| Missed 5 to 10 days | Restart the rotation from push | About 90% of previous loads for the first session of each type |
| Missed 2 to 4 weeks | Run one "re-entry" week with reduced volume | About 80 to 85% of previous loads, fewer sets |
| Missed 1 month or more | Treat it as a short rebuild over 2 to 3 weeks | Start around 70 to 80% and add weight each session |

These are practical starting points, not laws. Adjust based on how you feel in the first few sets.

### If you missed one day

This is by far the most common case. You planned pull on Tuesday, but work ran late.

**Do pull on Wednesday.** Legs moves to Thursday, and so on. If you run a six-day PPL with a fixed rest day, you have two options:

1. **Shift and lose the rest day once.** Train through Sunday that week and take your next rest day as normal. Fine if you're sleeping well and feel fresh.
2. **Shift and drop the last session of the week.** The rotation simply continues next week from wherever you stopped. Over a month, this evens out.

What you should **not** do is skip the pull session because the calendar says it's legs day. Do that a few times and you end up training legs once every ten days while pushing twice a week.

### If you missed several days (2 to 4)

Maybe you were sick for three days or travelling. Pick up exactly where you left off: if your last workout was push, your next one is pull.

Don't try to "catch up" by training twice a day or squeezing six sessions into four days. The extra fatigue costs more than the missed sessions. You'll usually find your strength is the same or even slightly better after a few days off, because you're well rested.

One exception: if you were ill, especially with a fever or chest symptoms, take it easy for the first session back and use lighter weights. If you're unsure whether you're recovered enough to train, check with a doctor.

### If you missed a week or more

After roughly a week off, it's cleaner to **restart the rotation from push** instead of trying to remember where you were. Your body won't care about the order, and starting fresh makes the week feel organised.

Expect the first session of each type to feel a bit rusty. You haven't lost meaningful muscle in a week or two, but your coordination and "groove" on heavy lifts take a session or two to return. Use slightly lighter loads (see the next section) and let the first week be a ramp, not a test.

## Merging sessions: when it makes sense

Sometimes you know in advance you'll only get three days in a week where you normally do six. In that case, merging two sessions can keep every muscle group trained.

### Option 1: switch to a 3-day PPL for the week

The simplest choice. Do push, pull, and legs once each, at full volume. You train each muscle once instead of twice, which is perfectly fine for a week or two.

### Option 2: merge into upper/lower or full body

If you'll only train twice that week:

- **Day 1: Upper** (main push lift + main pull lift + 2 to 3 accessories)
- **Day 2: Lower** (squat or leg press + a hinge like Romanian deadlift + calves or core)

Or, if you're down to one or two sessions, do **full body**: one squat or leg press, one press, one row or pull-down, and one hinge. Three working sets each is plenty.

### How to merge without wrecking the session

When you combine two sessions into one, don't just stack them end to end. A 90-minute workout late in the evening rarely goes well. Use these rules:

- **Keep the main compound lift from each session.** For example, bench press from push and barbell row from pull.
- **Cut accessories by about half.** If push normally has four accessories, keep two.
- **Drop sets on the "extras."** Three working sets on the main lifts, two on accessories.
- **Keep rest periods honest** on the heavy lifts. Our guide to [rest between sets for compound vs isolation exercises](/blog/rest-between-sets-compound-vs-isolation) explains why skimping on rest for big lifts usually backfires.

**Example merged push + pull session (about 60 minutes):**

| Exercise | Sets x reps | From |
|---|---|---|
| Bench press | 3 x 6 to 8 | Push |
| Barbell or chest-supported row | 3 x 8 to 10 | Pull |
| Seated dumbbell shoulder press | 2 x 8 to 12 | Push |
| Lat pull-down | 2 x 10 to 12 | Pull |
| Triceps pushdown + biceps curl | 2 x 12 to 15 each | Both |

Once the busy week is over, go back to your normal rotation. Don't permanently run merged sessions unless you've decided to change your split.

## How to resume your weights after time off

This is where most lifters either play it too safe (and waste weeks) or try to match their best numbers on day one (and feel crushed or get hurt). Here's a practical approach.

### After a few days off: no change

After 1 to 4 days off, use the same weights as your last session of that type. If you were progressing, keep progressing. If the first working set feels slow or heavy, hold the weight the same for that session rather than adding.

### After 1 to 2 weeks off: about 90%

Start each main lift at around 90% of your previous working weight and keep your usual reps. Then build back up over 2 to 3 sessions.

**Worked example: bench press**

- Before the break: 3 sets of 8 at **70 kg (155 lb)**
- First session back: 3 x 8 at **62.5 kg (about 140 lb)** (roughly 90%, rounded to a loadable weight)
- Second session: 3 x 8 at **67.5 kg (about 150 lb)**
- Third session: 3 x 8 at **70 kg (155 lb)**, back where you were

If the first session feels easy, jump straight to your old weight the next time. The aim is to confirm your groove, not to lose a month.

### After 2 to 4 weeks off: about 80 to 85%, fewer sets

Longer breaks hit your work capacity and joints more than your raw strength. Use lighter weights **and** reduce volume for the first week.

**Worked example: squat**

- Before the break: 4 sets of 6 at **100 kg (220 lb)**
- Week 1 back: 3 x 6 at **82.5 kg (about 180 lb)** (about 82%)
- Week 2: 3 x 6 at **90 kg (about 200 lb)**, then 4 x 6 at **95 kg (about 210 lb)**
- Week 3: 4 x 6 at **100 kg (220 lb)**

### After a month or more: rebuild over 2 to 3 weeks

Start around 70 to 80% of previous loads, with 2 to 3 working sets per exercise, and add weight every session as long as your reps are clean. Most people regain their previous strength faster than it took to build originally. Don't rush the first week; mild soreness after a long break can be intense, and getting too sore will cost you more sessions.

### Use rep targets to guide the climb

A double progression approach works well when returning: pick a rep range (say 6 to 10), and only add weight once you hit the top of the range on all sets. It naturally slows you down if you've lost a bit and speeds you up if you haven't. Our [double progression guide](/blog/double-progression) explains the method step by step.

## Examples: three real-world missed-day scenarios

### Scenario 1: six-day PPL, missed Wednesday legs

You train Monday push, Tuesday pull, Wednesday legs, and repeat, with Sunday off. A work deadline wipes out Wednesday.

- **Thursday:** legs (the missed session)
- **Friday:** push
- **Saturday:** pull
- **Sunday:** rest as usual
- **Monday:** legs, and the rotation continues

Net result: you did five sessions instead of six that week, and nothing got skipped twice in a row.

### Scenario 2: three-day PPL, sick for a week

You run push Monday, pull Wednesday, legs Friday. You catch a cold on the weekend and skip all of the following week.

- **Next Monday:** restart with push at about 90% of your last push loads
- **Wednesday:** pull at about 90%
- **Friday:** legs at about 90%
- **Following week:** back to full loads if everything felt solid

### Scenario 3: rolling PPL, irregular weeks

You train whenever you can, usually 4 to 5 times a week, in a push, pull, legs rotation. You trained push on Sunday, then couldn't get to the gym until Wednesday.

- **Wednesday:** pull (the next session after push)
- Then legs, then push, as your schedule allows

This is exactly why rolling schedules suit irregular routines: there's nothing to "fix." You just do the next one.

## Keep track of where you are in the rotation

The biggest practical problem with any PPL, especially a rolling one, is remembering **what you did last** and **what weights you used**. After a few days off, it's easy to forget whether you did push or pull last, or whether your row was at 60 kg or 65 kg.

A log solves both. Whether it's a notebook or an app, write down the session name, the exercises, and the sets, reps, and weight. ## Common mistakes after a PPL missed day

- **Skipping the missed session to "stay on schedule."** This creates imbalances over time. Legs is the session most often skipped this way.
- **Doubling up the next day.** Two full sessions back to back, or one giant combined session, leaves you exhausted and usually lowers the quality of both.
- **Restarting the whole program.** Unless you were off for weeks, you don't need to go back to week one of anything.
- **Testing your max on the first day back.** Ease in. Your strength will return quickly if you let it.
- **Treating one bad week as failure.** Consistency is measured over months. A missed day is a normal part of training, not a sign the plan isn't working.

## Putting it into practice

Here's the whole guide in a few lines you can save:

1. **Missed one day?** Do that session next and shift everything forward.
2. **Missed a few days?** Resume where you left off at the same weights.
3. **Missed a week or more?** Restart from push at about 90% and build back over 2 to 3 sessions.
4. **Missed several weeks?** Use about 80% loads and fewer sets for a week, then ramp up.
5. **Short on days this week?** Merge sessions into upper/lower or full body, keeping the main lifts and trimming accessories.
6. **Log every session** so you always know what's next and what you lifted.

If you've missed a day on push pull legs more than once this month and you're on a fixed weekly calendar, consider switching to a rolling order with one fixed rest day. It's one of the easiest changes you can make to a PPL, and it turns "I missed a day" from a problem into a non-event.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I skip a missed workout or do it the next day?

Do it the next day. Skipping the missed session means that muscle group gets trained less often than the others, and if it keeps happening, you build imbalances. Shifting the whole rotation forward by one day keeps every session in order. Over a month, losing one training day matters very little, while losing an entire session type, like legs, adds up quickly.

### Can I do two push pull legs workouts in one day to catch up?

You can, but it's rarely worth it. Two full sessions in a day piles on fatigue and usually lowers the quality of the second workout. If you're short on time for the week, it's better to merge sessions by keeping the main compound lift from each and cutting accessories in half. Then return to your normal rotation once your schedule settles.

### Is a rotating split better than a weekly schedule for PPL?

Neither is better for results; they suit different lifestyles. A weekly schedule is easy to plan if your weeks are predictable. A rotating split handles missed days automatically because you always do the next session in order. Many lifters use a hybrid: a rolling push, pull, legs order with one fixed rest day each week for predictability.

### How much strength do you lose after missing a week at the gym?

Very little for most people. After a week off, strength typically feels slightly rusty for one or two sessions, mostly because of coordination and timing rather than lost muscle. Starting at around 90% of your previous working weights and building back over two or three sessions is usually enough to get back to where you were.

### What if I keep missing the same day every week?

That's a sign your schedule doesn't fit your life, not that you lack discipline. Move the session that keeps getting missed to a day you reliably train, switch to a rolling rotation, or reduce your plan from six days to four or five. A plan you can follow consistently will beat a more ambitious plan you keep breaking.
