Body recomposition means losing fat and gaining muscle at the same time, often while your scale weight barely moves. The protein intake for body recomposition that the research supports is about **1.6-2.4 g per kg of body weight a day (0.7-1.1 g per lb)**, paired with progressive resistance training and calories at maintenance or 10-20% below it. It works best for beginners, people returning after a layoff and people with more fat to lose. Lean, experienced lifters can still recomp, but their changes are small and slow.

Protein is the lever most people get wrong, so it gets its own section here. The full case for how much protein builds muscle lives in our guide to [how much protein to build muscle](/blog/how-much-protein-to-build-muscle). This post covers the rest: who body recomp suits, how to set calories, the training that makes it happen, how to measure it when the scale won't help, and when to stop and bulk or cut instead.

## What body recomposition is

Body recomposition is a change in what your body weight is made of. A [2024 editorial in Frontiers in Nutrition](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11405322/) defines it as losing body fat while keeping or adding lean mass, often with no change in total body weight. Lifters shorten it to "body recomp" or just "recomp".

The textbook model says muscle gain needs a calorie surplus and fat loss needs a deficit, so you can't have both at once. That holds up well for lean lifters with years of training behind them. For many other people it's too strict: a new training stimulus plus plenty of protein can build muscle while stored fat covers part of the energy bill.

Recomp has no special diet and needs no supplements. It comes down to calories near maintenance, high protein, hard training that gets progressively harder, and the patience to measure it properly.

### Recomp compared with bulking and cutting

| Approach | Calories | Scale weight | Main goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bulk | Above maintenance | Rises slowly | Build as much muscle as possible, accept some fat |
| Cut | Clearly below maintenance | Falls | Lose fat, keep the muscle you have |
| Recomp | Maintenance to about 20% below | Flat or drifting down | Lose fat and gain muscle together |

## Who body recomp works for

A [2026 review in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13757920/) (Naddafha, Schoenfeld, Antonio and Stout) found that recomposition effects are largest in untrained people and people with higher body fat, and smaller in trained or lean athletes. Here is how that plays out in practice.

| You are | Recomp odds | Why |
|---|---|---|
| New to lifting (less than a year of structured training) | High | Muscle responds quickly to a training stimulus it hasn't had before |
| Returning after a long break | High | Lifters coming back usually regain size and strength faster than they built it the first time |
| Carrying more body fat (the review's cut-off is about 20%+ for men, 30%+ for women) | High | Stored fat can fund muscle gain while you eat below maintenance |
| Training for a while without a real plan | Moderate to high | Adding progressive overload and enough weekly sets is a new stimulus, even if the gym isn't new to you |
| Lean and experienced (about 12% or less for men, 22% or less for women) | Low | Little stored energy to draw on, and muscle gain is slow at this stage |

The fourth row is the one people overlook. If you've been going to the gym for two years but doing the same weights for the same reps every week, your body has had no reason to change. Fix the training and you get some of the "beginner" response back.

## What the research says about body recomposition

### The 2026 review

The JISSN review pulled together randomised trials and meta-analyses on adults. Its main conclusions:

- **Progressive resistance training plus high protein** is the most consistent recipe, with protein typically around 1.6-2.4 g/kg a day and higher when you're lean or dieting.
- **Deficits should be moderate**: roughly 10-20% below maintenance, with trained lifters losing no more than about 0.5-0.7% of body weight per week.
- **Training volume** of about 10-20 sets per major muscle group per week, spread over two or three sessions, is a reasonable range.
- **Monitoring** should combine a body composition measure with strength and performance, and calories, protein and training should be adjusted when the trends drift.

The review also gives a practical table of the changes you might expect over 12-16 weeks. Treat these as the authors' estimates for each group, not as promises.

| Starting point | Lean mass change | Fat mass change |
|---|---|---|
| Novice | +1 to 3 kg (2-7 lb) | -2 to 6 kg (4-13 lb) |
| Trained | 0 to +1.5 kg (0-3 lb) | -1 to 3 kg (2-7 lb) |
| Higher body fat | 0 to +3 kg (0-7 lb) | -4 to 10 kg (9-22 lb) |
| Lean | -0.5 to +0.5 kg (-1 to +1 lb) | -0.5 to 2 kg (1-4 lb) |

Look at the trained and lean rows before you start. A lean lifter who recomps perfectly for three months might add half a kilo of muscle, which no mirror will show you.

### A 2026 trial in trained lifters

A [10-week study in the European Journal of Applied Physiology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13380550/) (Vargas-Molina and colleagues) took 30 people with at least a year of lifting experience, mostly young men, and had them train four days a week. One group ate at maintenance, another ate 250 kcal below it, and both ate 2.5 g of protein per kg. A control group did the same programme on its own with no diet guidance.

Measured by DEXA scan:

| Group | Fat-free mass | Fat mass |
|---|---|---|
| Maintenance, 2.5 g/kg protein | +0.97 kg (+2.1 lb) | -1.41 kg (-3.1 lb) |
| 250 kcal deficit, 2.5 g/kg protein | +1.04 kg (+2.3 lb) | -2.94 kg (-6.5 lb) |
| Same training, no diet guidance | No meaningful change | No meaningful change |

It's a small study, so don't read too much into the exact numbers. Still, it shows two useful things: trained lifters can recomp, and the group that trained without the nutrition plan got nowhere. You need both halves.

### Protein dose matters at any age

The Frontiers editorial summarises a trial in untrained men over 60 doing eight weeks of resistance training, where 1.6 g/kg of protein a day beat 0.8 g/kg for gains in estimated muscle mass and one-rep max strength. The editorial also notes that recomposition has been shown in untrained, trained and highly trained groups of different ages.

## Protein intake for body recomposition: how much to eat

The research range for protein intake for body recomposition is wide, so pick a spot in it based on your situation:

- **1.6 g/kg (0.7 g/lb)**: a sensible floor, fine for beginners eating near maintenance.
- **2.0 g/kg (0.9 g/lb)**: a good default for most people in a small deficit.
- **2.2-2.4 g/kg (1.0-1.1 g/lb)**: if you're lean, experienced, or running a bigger deficit. The review goes higher still for very lean trained lifters.

### Daily protein by body weight

| Body weight | 1.6 g/kg (0.7 g/lb) | 2.0 g/kg (0.9 g/lb) | 2.4 g/kg (1.1 g/lb) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 kg (132 lb) | 96 g | 120 g | 144 g |
| 70 kg (154 lb) | 112 g | 140 g | 168 g |
| 80 kg (176 lb) | 128 g | 160 g | 192 g |
| 90 kg (198 lb) | 144 g | 180 g | 216 g |
| 100 kg (220 lb) | 160 g | 200 g | 240 g |

If you carry a lot of body fat, base the number on your goal weight instead. Someone at 110 kg (243 lb) aiming for 90 kg (198 lb) would use 90 kg: 144 g a day at 1.6 g/kg rather than 176 g. That's easier to eat and still covers what the muscle needs.

### Spread it across the day

The review suggests about 0.3-0.5 g/kg per meal. For an 80 kg (176 lb) lifter, that's 24-40 g per meal, so four meals of about 40 g reach 160 g. Rest days count too: muscle is built in the days after training, so keep the target the same every day.

| Meal | Example | Protein (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| Breakfast | 3 eggs and 200 g (7 oz) Greek yogurt | about 38 g |
| Lunch | 150 g (5.3 oz) cooked chicken breast with rice | about 45 g |
| Snack | 1 scoop whey in 300 ml milk | about 30-35 g, depending on the powder |
| Dinner | 180 g (6.3 oz) salmon fillet with potatoes | about 35 g |
| **Total** | | **about 150-155 g** |

Protein values are rounded and packaged foods vary, so check the label. Missing the number on one day doesn't matter much; hitting it most days for twelve weeks does.

## Calories for body recomp: maintenance or a small deficit

### Step 1: find your maintenance

Calculators give you a starting guess, but your own data is better. Eat normally and log it for two to three weeks while you weigh yourself each morning. If your weekly average weight stays flat, your average daily intake over that stretch is your maintenance. Our guide on [how often to weigh yourself](/blog/how-often-to-weigh-yourself) shows how to build that weekly average.

### Step 2: pick a deficit that fits

Based on the review's practical table:

| Your situation | Calorie target | Weekly weight change |
|---|---|---|
| Lean and experienced | Maintenance to 10% below | Flat to -0.5% of body weight |
| Trained, average body fat | 5-15% below maintenance | -0.25 to -0.5% |
| Beginner, average body fat | 10-20% below maintenance | about -0.5 to -0.7% |
| Higher body fat | 15-25% below maintenance | -0.7 to -1.0% |

### Worked example in kg

An 80 kg lifter with a year of training, average body fat, and a maintenance of 2,600 kcal:

- **Calories:** 10% below maintenance = 2,340 kcal a day.
- **Target rate:** 0.25-0.5% of body weight = 0.2-0.4 kg per week.
- **Protein:** 2.0 g/kg = 160 g (640 kcal).
- **Fat:** 0.8 g/kg = 64 g (576 kcal). The review suggests keeping fat at or above 0.6-0.8 g/kg.
- **Carbs:** the rest. 2,340 - 640 - 576 = 1,124 kcal, or about 280 g.

### Worked example in lb

A 220 lb (100 kg) beginner with higher body fat, a maintenance of 3,000 kcal, and a goal weight of 190 lb (86 kg):

- **Calories:** 20% below maintenance = 2,400 kcal a day.
- **Target rate:** 0.7-1.0% of body weight = 1.5-2.2 lb (0.7-1.0 kg) per week.
- **Protein:** 1.6-2.0 g/kg of goal weight = about 138-172 g. Call it 160 g (640 kcal).
- **Fat:** 70 g (630 kcal).
- **Carbs:** 2,400 - 640 - 630 = 1,130 kcal, or about 280 g.

Some lifters eat a bit more on training days and less on rest days, keeping the weekly total the same. The review notes that a little more carbohydrate on key training days can help sessions go well. It's optional. Hitting your weekly calories and daily protein matters far more than how you slice them.

What doesn't work is a crash diet with a recomp label. Big deficits make muscle gain much harder, and you end up cutting with extra steps.

## Resistance training for body recomposition

The diet makes room for recomp, and the training is what actually builds the muscle. Without lifting, a deficit just makes you a smaller version of yourself. The trial's control group is the reminder: the same programme without the nutrition plan produced no meaningful change.

### Progressive overload

Your lifts need to go up, or at least hold, while you're near maintenance. The review treats the ability to keep or increase your loads during a deficit as a sign the plan is working. Log every session so you can see it; our guide on [how to track progressive overload](/blog/how-to-track-progressive-overload) shows what to record and how to read it.

A simple way to progress is [double progression](/blog/double-progression): work up to the top of a rep range across all sets, then add weight. Here's a beginner's bench press over six weeks of a recomp:

| Week | Weight | Reps (3 sets) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 60 kg (132 lb) | 8, 8, 7 |
| 2 | 60 kg (132 lb) | 8, 8, 8 |
| 3 | 62.5 kg (138 lb) | 8, 7, 6 |
| 4 | 62.5 kg (138 lb) | 8, 8, 7 |
| 5 | 62.5 kg (138 lb) | 8, 8, 8 |
| 6 | 65 kg (143 lb) | 8, 7, 7 |

That's 5 kg (11 lb) on the bar in six weeks while eating below maintenance. Experienced lifters will move more slowly, and holding steady can count as a win.

### Enough weekly sets

The review suggests about 10-20 hard sets per major muscle group per week, spread over two or three sessions. Beginners respond well at the lower end. It also notes that, in a deficit, higher volumes didn't beat moderate volumes for keeping muscle in a recent trial, so piling on sets isn't the answer. Our guide to [how many sets per muscle group per week](/blog/how-many-sets-per-muscle-group-per-week) covers how to count them.

| Muscle group | Beginner (sets per week) | Experienced (sets per week) | Example split |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chest | 10 | 14 | Two sessions: press plus a fly or second press |
| Back | 10-12 | 16 | Two sessions: a row plus a pulldown or pull-up |
| Quads | 10 | 14 | Two sessions: squat or leg press plus a lunge |
| Hamstrings and glutes | 8-10 | 12 | Two sessions: a hinge plus a leg curl |
| Shoulders | 8 | 12 | Presses count partly; add lateral raises |
| Arms | 6-8 each | 10 each | Curls and triceps work twice a week |

Take most sets to within one to three reps of failure. Sets that end with five easy reps left in the tank don't count for much.

### Cardio

Cardio helps with fat loss and health, but too much eats into recovery. The review's table for beginners suggests two or three short cardio sessions a week plus a daily step target, done after lifting or a few hours apart from it. A daily walk is the easiest win here.

## How to measure body recomposition when the scale barely moves

On a good recomp the scale can look broken. Fat is coming off, muscle is going on, and the two cancel out. You need other measures:

1. **Scale weight, as a weekly average.** Weigh every morning and average each week. You're looking for a flat or slowly falling line, not a single number.
2. **Waist measurement.** Measure at your navel, first thing in the morning, relaxed, every one to two weeks. A shrinking waist with a stable weight is the clearest sign of recomp you can get at home.
3. **Photos every four weeks.** Same light, same spot, same time of day. Compare month to month, never day to day.
4. **Your lifts.** Rising loads or reps on the same exercises mean you're holding or building muscle.

A DEXA scan every 8-12 weeks gives you actual numbers if you want them and can pay for them. Home body-fat scales are less useful for this: their readings shift with how much water you're holding, which can swamp a change of a few hundred grams.

### A 12-week example

An 80 kg lifter eating about 5% under maintenance with 160 g of protein a day:

| Week | Average weight | Waist | Bench top set | Squat top set |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 80.0 kg (176.4 lb) | 88.0 cm (34.6 in) | 70 kg (154 lb) x 6 | 90 kg (198 lb) x 6 |
| 4 | 79.4 kg (175.0 lb) | 87.0 cm (34.3 in) | 72.5 kg (160 lb) x 6 | 95 kg (209 lb) x 6 |
| 8 | 79.0 kg (174.2 lb) | 86.0 cm (33.9 in) | 75 kg (165 lb) x 6 | 100 kg (220 lb) x 6 |
| 12 | 78.7 kg (173.5 lb) | 85.0 cm (33.5 in) | 77.5 kg (171 lb) x 6 | 102.5 kg (226 lb) x 6 |

Judged by the scale alone, this lifter lost 1.3 kg (2.9 lb) in 12 weeks and might think the diet failed. Add the other columns and the waist is down 3 cm (1.2 in), the bench is up 7.5 kg (17 lb) and the squat is up 12.5 kg (28 lb). That's a recomp working.

It helps to keep these in one place. GymLedger has a body-weight chart and strength charts for every exercise in the same app, so you can read the scale trend and your lifts side by side.

### Reading the signals

| Scale (weekly average) | Waist | Lifts | What it likely means | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flat or slowly down | Down | Up | Recomp is working | Change nothing |
| Flat | Flat | Up | Building muscle, little fat loss | Fine if you're lean; otherwise trim 100-200 kcal |
| Flat | Flat | Flat for 3+ weeks | Not much is happening | Check overload, weekly sets and protein before cutting food |
| Rising | Rising | Up | You're in a surplus | Bulk on purpose, or trim calories |
| Falling fast | Down | Falling | Deficit is too big | Add 150-250 kcal a day |

## How long body recomposition takes

Recomp is slow by design. Rough expectations for someone who trains and eats consistently:

| Timeframe | What you'll probably notice |
|---|---|
| Weeks 1-2 | Scale noise from water and glycogen; strength rises, mostly from better technique |
| Weeks 3-6 | Steady rep and load increases in your log; the waist starts to edge down if you're in a deficit |
| Weeks 8-12 | Side-by-side photos start to show it, and clothes fit differently |
| Weeks 12-16 | A fair point to judge the result against the review's estimates for your group |
| 6 months and beyond | Beginners and people with more fat to lose can often keep going; lean, trained lifters usually stall sooner |

If you've given it 8-12 weeks of honest tracking and none of the measures have moved, the plan needs changing, not more patience.

## When to switch to a bulk or a cut

Recomp is one tool, not a lifestyle. Our guide to [bulking vs cutting](/blog/bulking-vs-cutting) covers both in depth, but here's when to make the switch:

| Situation | Better choice |
|---|---|
| Lifts rising, waist shrinking, weight flat or slowly down | Keep recomping |
| Lean (about 12% body fat or less for men, 22% or less for women) and mainly want size | Bulk |
| Lifts stalled for 3-4 weeks despite good sleep, enough sets and enough protein | Bulk, or take a [deload](/blog/deload-week) and reassess |
| A lot of fat to lose and you want it gone faster | Cut, with protein kept high |
| Event or deadline coming up | Cut |
| You hate tracking and want one simple direction | Pick a slow bulk or a slow cut |

A common pattern is to recomp for 3-6 months as a beginner, then move into alternating lean bulks and short cuts once progress slows.

## Common body recomp mistakes

- **Running a crash diet and calling it recomp.** A deficit of 30% or more doesn't leave much room to build anything.
- **Hitting protein on training days only.** Muscle is built in the days after the session, so rest days need the same target.
- **Programme hopping.** Change exercises every two weeks and you can't tell whether you're getting stronger.
- **Judging by the morning scale.** One salty dinner can add a kilo of water. Use weekly averages.
- **Adding cardio until recovery suffers.** If your lifts are sliding, cut cardio before you cut food.
- **Not logging lifts.** Without a log, you're guessing which of the five measures is moving.

## Putting it into practice

1. **Find maintenance:** two to three weeks of normal eating, logged, with daily weigh-ins.
2. **Set calories:** maintenance to 25% below, using the table above for your situation.
3. **Set protein:** a protein intake for body recomposition of 1.6-2.4 g/kg a day, based on goal weight if you carry a lot of fat, split over three to five meals.
4. **Train hard:** 10-20 sets per muscle per week, close to failure, adding weight or reps when you can.
5. **Track weekly:** scale average every week, waist every one to two weeks, photos every four, lifts every session.
6. **Review at 8-12 weeks:** keep going, or switch to a bulk or cut using the table above.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you build muscle in a calorie deficit?

Yes, especially if you're new to lifting, returning after a break or carrying more body fat. In a 2026 trial, lifters with at least a year of training gained about 1 kg of fat-free mass in 10 weeks while eating 250 kcal below maintenance with 2.5 g of protein per kg. Keep the deficit moderate and protein high, and keep adding load or reps. Large deficits make muscle gain much harder.

### What is the right protein intake for body recomposition?

Aim for about 1.6-2.4 g per kg of body weight a day (0.7-1.1 g per lb). Use the lower end near maintenance and the upper end if you're lean or in a bigger deficit. An 80 kg (176 lb) lifter would eat roughly 130-190 g a day, spread over three to five meals. If you carry a lot of body fat, base the number on your goal weight instead.

### Should I eat at maintenance or in a deficit for a body recomp?

Both can work. Lean, experienced lifters often do best at maintenance or slightly below it, because there's little fat to fund muscle gain. Most other people do well 10-20% below maintenance, and people with more body fat can go a little lower. In one 2026 trial, a 250 kcal deficit roughly doubled fat loss compared with maintenance while lean mass rose by about the same amount.

### How long does body recomposition take?

Expect strength to rise within a few weeks, the waist to start shrinking within a month or two, and visible changes in photos around 8-12 weeks. A 2026 review estimated that over 12-16 weeks, beginners might gain 1-3 kg (2-7 lb) of lean mass and lose 2-6 kg (4-13 lb) of fat, while lean, trained lifters see much smaller changes. Judge it over months, not weeks.

### Does body recomp work if you're skinny fat?

Usually, yes. "Skinny fat" tends to mean low muscle and moderate body fat, which describes many beginners, the group that responds best to recomp. Eat around maintenance or slightly below, get 1.6-2.2 g of protein per kg, and train each muscle with progressive overload two or three times a week. Expect the scale to barely move while your waist shrinks and your lifts climb.
