Here is the double progression method explained in one line: pick a rep range, keep the weight the same until you hit the top of the range on every set, then add a small amount of weight and start again from the bottom. You progress reps first and weight second, which is why it is called "double" progression. It works for almost any exercise and needs nothing more than an honest log.

This guide covers how the method works, how to choose a double progression rep range, a full multi-week example in kg and lb, how to handle big dumbbell jumps, and when it is time to switch to something else.

## What is double progression?

Double progression is a way of applying progressive overload using two variables in sequence: **reps, then load**.

1. Choose a rep range, for example **8 to 12 reps**.
2. Choose a weight you can lift for at least the bottom of that range on all your sets.
3. Each session, try to add reps at the same weight.
4. When you reach the top of the range on **every** working set, add weight.
5. The reps drop back toward the bottom of the range. Repeat.

That is the whole system. There is no percentage chart and no complicated cycle. Your previous session tells you what to do in the next one.

### Why it works so well

Single progression, where you add weight every session, works for beginners. It breaks down once the smallest available weight jump is bigger than the strength you can gain between two sessions.

Double progression solves that by giving you a smaller step to take. Instead of needing to lift 2.5 kg (5 lb) more today, you only need one more rep on one set. Those small rep gains add up until the heavier weight is manageable.

It also has a built-in safety margin. You only add weight after proving you own the current one across all sets. That makes big, ego-driven jumps much less likely.

## The double progression method explained step by step

Let's walk through the decision you make for each exercise, every session. This is the core of the method, so it is worth getting precise.

### Step 1: Set the range and the number of sets

Example: dumbbell bench press, **3 sets of 8 to 12 reps**.

### Step 2: Pick a starting weight

Choose a weight where you can get **at least 8 reps on every set** with good form, but not much more than 10 on the first set. If you get 12 or more on the first set, the weight is too light. If you cannot get 8, it is too heavy.

### Step 3: Each session, apply this rule

| Last session result | Next session action |
|---|---|
| All sets at the top of the range (12, 12, 12) | **Add weight** |
| Some sets below the top | **Same weight**, aim for more reps |
| Any set below the bottom of the range | **Same weight** if it was a bad day; reduce weight if it happens twice |

### Step 4: Add the smallest available weight

When you earn a weight increase, use the smallest jump your equipment allows. For a barbell that is usually 2.5 kg (5 lb) total. For dumbbells it is often 2 kg or 2.5 kg per hand in kg gyms, or 5 lb per hand in lb gyms. For machines, one pin on the stack.

Then go back to Step 3. You will likely land near the bottom of the range, and the climb begins again.

## Double progression example: 8 weeks in kg and lb

Here is a realistic double progression example for a barbell bench press, 3 sets of **6 to 10 reps**, trained once per week. The lifter adds 2.5 kg (5 lb) when all three sets reach 10.

### Kilograms

| Week | Weight | Set 1 | Set 2 | Set 3 | Total reps | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 60 kg | 9 | 8 | 7 | 24 | Same weight |
| 2 | 60 kg | 10 | 9 | 8 | 27 | Same weight |
| 3 | 60 kg | 10 | 10 | 9 | 29 | Same weight |
| 4 | 60 kg | 10 | 10 | 10 | 30 | **Add 2.5 kg** |
| 5 | 62.5 kg | 8 | 7 | 7 | 22 | Same weight |
| 6 | 62.5 kg | 9 | 8 | 8 | 25 | Same weight |
| 7 | 62.5 kg | 10 | 9 | 9 | 28 | Same weight |
| 8 | 62.5 kg | 10 | 10 | 10 | 30 | **Add 2.5 kg** |

### Pounds

| Week | Weight | Set 1 | Set 2 | Set 3 | Total reps | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 135 lb | 9 | 8 | 7 | 24 | Same weight |
| 2 | 135 lb | 10 | 9 | 8 | 27 | Same weight |
| 3 | 135 lb | 10 | 10 | 9 | 29 | Same weight |
| 4 | 135 lb | 10 | 10 | 10 | 30 | **Add 5 lb** |
| 5 | 140 lb | 8 | 7 | 7 | 22 | Same weight |
| 6 | 140 lb | 9 | 8 | 8 | 25 | Same weight |
| 7 | 140 lb | 10 | 9 | 9 | 28 | Same weight |
| 8 | 140 lb | 10 | 10 | 10 | 30 | **Add 5 lb** |

### What this example shows

- **Progress happens every week**, even when the weight does not change. Total reps went 24, 27, 29, 30.
- **The weight went up twice in eight weeks.** That is 5 kg (10 lb) on the bar, at full rep targets, which is solid progress for an intermediate.
- **Reps drop after each jump.** Week 5 looks like a step backward, but it is the expected reset.
- **Not every week will look this neat.** Real logs have repeat weeks and the occasional dip. The pattern over a month is what matters.

Volume tells the same story. Week 4 at 60 kg is 30 x 60 = 1,800 kg. Week 8 at 62.5 kg is 30 x 62.5 = 1,875 kg. In pounds, week 4 is 30 x 135 = 4,050 lb and week 8 is 30 x 140 = 4,200 lb. Same reps, more weight: that is overload.

## How to choose a double progression rep range

The double progression rep range you pick depends mainly on the exercise. Big compound lifts tolerate heavier loads and lower reps. Small isolation exercises usually do better with lighter loads and higher reps.

| Exercise type | Suggested range | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Heavy compounds** | 4 to 6, or 5 to 8 | Squat, deadlift, bench press, overhead press |
| **Secondary compounds** | 6 to 10, or 8 to 12 | Rows, incline press, Romanian deadlift, leg press |
| **Isolation** | 10 to 15, or 12 to 20 | Lateral raises, curls, triceps extensions, leg curls |
| **Bodyweight** | Rep range, then add load | Pull-ups 5 to 10, dips 8 to 12 |

These are starting points, not rules. The American College of Sports Medicine's [position stand on resistance training progression](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/) suggests an 8 to 12 repetition range for novices and a wider range for more experienced lifters, which fits this approach well.

### Width of the range matters

A useful guideline: **make the range wide enough that one weight jump fits inside it**.

On a 100 kg (225 lb) squat, a 2.5 kg (5 lb) jump is about 2.5 percent, so a narrow range like 5 to 7 is enough. On a 10 kg (20 lb) dumbbell curl, the next dumbbell might be 25 percent heavier, so you need a wide range like 10 to 15 or 12 to 20 to absorb the drop in reps.

If the range is too narrow for the jump, you add weight and fall below the bottom of the range. That is the most common reason double progression seems to stall.

### Compounds: lower and narrower

On compound lifts, keep the range fairly low and fairly narrow. High-rep sets of squats and deadlifts tend to break down because of breathing, grip, and general fatigue before the target muscles are trained hard. A 5 to 8 range on squats is easier to execute well than a 12 to 20 range.

Rest matters too. Heavy compound sets need more time between them if you want to keep reps up across all three sets. Our guide to [rest between sets for compound vs isolation exercises](/blog/rest-between-sets-compound-vs-isolation) explains the trade-off.

### Isolation: higher and wider

Isolation exercises like lateral raises and curls use small muscles and small weights. Equipment jumps are large in percentage terms, so wider and higher rep ranges are the practical choice. They are also easier on joints for many lifters.

## How to add weight with double progression

Knowing how to add weight with double progression is mostly about choosing the right jump size and the right trigger.

### The trigger

The cleanest trigger is **all sets at the top of the range, with good form**. Some lifters use a looser trigger, such as "first set hits the top" or "total reps reach a target." Those work too, but the all-sets rule is the most conservative and the easiest to follow from a log.

### The jump size

| Equipment | Typical smallest jump (kg gym) | Typical smallest jump (lb gym) |
|---|---|---|
| Barbell | 2.5 kg (two 1.25 kg plates) | 5 lb (two 2.5 lb plates) |
| Barbell with micro plates | 1 kg (two 0.5 kg plates) | 2 lb (two 1 lb plates) |
| Dumbbells | 2 to 2.5 kg per hand | 5 lb per hand |
| Weight stack machine | One pin, often 5 kg | One pin, often 10 lb |

Always use the smallest jump you have, especially on upper-body lifts. If plate math in a kg gym is new to you, our guide on [how to load a barbell in kg](/blog/how-to-load-a-barbell-in-kg) covers the common plate combinations.

### Different sets, different weights

Some lifters handle a weight jump one set at a time. For example, after hitting 3 x 10 at 60 kg, they do set 1 at 62.5 kg and sets 2 and 3 at 60 kg. Over the next sessions, more sets move to the heavier weight. This smooths out the jump and works well for lifters who find a full reset frustrating. Just log each set's weight accurately.

## The small-increment problem with dumbbells

Dumbbells are where double progression gets tricky. Many gyms jump in 2 kg or 2.5 kg steps per hand, or 5 lb steps in pound gyms. On light exercises, that is a huge relative increase.

### Why the jump hurts

Take a lateral raise:

- **kg:** going from 8 kg to 10 kg per hand is a 25 percent increase.
- **lb:** going from 15 lb to 20 lb per hand is a 33 percent increase.

If you were doing 3 x 15 at 8 kg, you might only manage 8 or 9 reps at 10 kg. With a range of 10 to 15, you have fallen out of the bottom.

### Fixes that work

1. **Widen the range.** Use 12 to 20 or even 15 to 25 on small isolation lifts. A wide range means the rep drop after a jump still lands inside it.
2. **Go past the top before jumping.** On isolation work, it is fine to push to 18 or 20 reps before moving up, even if your nominal range is 12 to 15.
3. **Add a set before adding weight.** Move from 3 sets to 4 at the same weight, then jump and drop back to 3.
4. **Move one set up at a time.** Do your first set with the heavier dumbbell and the rest with the lighter one until all sets reach the bottom of the range.
5. **Use add-on weights if your gym has them.** Small magnetic add-on plates for dumbbells exist and help a lot, but not every gym stocks them.
6. **Switch to a cable or machine** for that muscle if the stack has finer steps.

Also decide once whether you record dumbbells per hand or as a total, and keep it consistent so your history stays comparable. We cover this in [how to log dumbbells: per hand or total](/blog/log-dumbbells-per-hand-or-total).

### Dumbbell example: incline press in kg and lb

Here is a double progression example on a dumbbell incline press, 3 sets of 8 to 12, with a 2 kg (5 lb) per-hand jump and a "one set at a time" approach.

| Session | kg (per hand) | lb (per hand) | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 x 12, 12, 12 | 45 x 12, 12, 12 | Earned the jump |
| 2 | 22 x 9, then 20 x 12, 11 | 50 x 9, then 45 x 12, 11 | Set 1 heavier |
| 3 | 22 x 10, 22 x 8, 20 x 11 | 50 x 10, 50 x 8, 45 x 11 | Two sets heavier |
| 4 | 22 x 10, 9, 8 | 50 x 10, 9, 8 | All sets at new weight |
| 5 | 22 x 11, 10, 9 | 50 x 11, 10, 9 | Build reps |

Without the staggered approach, session 2 might have been 22 kg for 9, 7, 6, which falls below the range on two sets. The staggered version keeps every set productive.

## Tracking double progression in your log

Once you have the double progression method explained on paper, it lives or dies on your records. You need to know exactly what you did on each set last session, or you cannot make the next decision.

For each exercise, record the **weight and reps for every working set**, plus the date. Keep the rep range visible, either at the top of the page in a notebook or in your workout template. Before each exercise, look at the last session and apply the rule from the table above.

If you use GymLedger, you can store the rep range for each exercise in your workout template, log the weight and reps for each set quickly, and check the per-exercise progress chart to see the weight steps add up over time. ## Common double progression mistakes

### Starting too heavy

If you begin at the bottom of the range and stall there, you have no room to progress. Start with a weight that puts you comfortably inside the range on the first set.

### Counting sloppy reps

Reps that need a big swing or a shortened range of motion are not the same reps you did at a lighter weight. Keep a consistent technique standard, or your log will tell you to add weight you are not ready for.

### Using one range for everything

A 6 to 8 range on lateral raises or a 15 to 20 range on deadlifts both cause problems. Match the range to the exercise and to the size of the available weight jump.

### Jumping too far

Skipping from 60 kg to 65 kg (135 lb to 145 lb) on bench because "it felt easy" resets you too far down. Use the smallest jump and let the system work.

## When to switch methods

Double progression can carry most lifters a long way. Still, there are signs it is time to change approach or add structure around it.

### Signs it is time to change

- **You stall at the same weight for 3 to 4 weeks** across several lifts, even after checking sleep, food, and rest.
- **Rep gains have become tiny and inconsistent**, with weeks going up and down rather than steadily rising.
- **You want to peak strength for a specific goal**, such as a powerlifting meet or a 1-rep max test, which calls for more specific, lower-rep planning.

### What to try instead

1. **Take a deload first.** A lighter week often restarts progress without changing anything else. Many apparent plateaus are just accumulated fatigue.
2. **Change the rep range.** Moving a lift from 6 to 10 to 4 to 6, or the reverse, gives a new stimulus and a fresh set of rep targets.
3. **Change the exercise variation** for a training block and run double progression on the new lift.
4. **Add a structured program.** Intermediates sometimes move to programs with planned weekly changes in load and reps (often called periodization). Double progression can still run on accessory lifts inside those programs.

If your plateau is tied to your schedule, the issue may be your split rather than the method. Our guide to [building your own workout split](/blog/how-to-build-your-own-workout-split) helps you rebalance frequency and volume.

## Putting it into practice

With the double progression method explained, here is how to start it in your next workout.

1. **Assign a rep range to each exercise.** Use 5 to 8 for heavy compounds, 8 to 12 for secondary compounds, and 12 to 20 for isolation.
2. **Pick a starting weight** that gets you into the middle of the range on your first set.
3. **Log every working set.** Weight and reps, every time.
4. **Apply the rule.** All sets at the top of the range means add the smallest jump. Otherwise, same weight and aim for one more rep.
5. **Fix dumbbell jumps** with wider ranges, extra sets, or moving one set up at a time.
6. **Review every month.** If the weight on a lift has not moved in three or four weeks, deload or change one variable.

The method is simple enough to run from a notebook, and an app like [GymLedger](/) makes the history easier to check between sets. Either way, the double progression method only asks one thing of you: beat last time by a little.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the double progression method?

Double progression is a way to apply progressive overload by increasing two variables in order. First, you keep the weight the same and add reps within a set range, such as 8 to 12. When you reach the top of the range on every working set, you add a small amount of weight and drop back toward the bottom of the range. Then you repeat the process with the new weight.

### What is a good rep range for double progression?

For heavy compound lifts like squats and bench press, ranges such as 4 to 6 or 5 to 8 work well. For secondary compounds like rows and leg press, try 8 to 12. For isolation exercises like curls and lateral raises, use wider ranges such as 12 to 20. The key is choosing a range wide enough that a single weight jump still lands inside it.

### How much weight should I add with double progression?

Add the smallest increment your equipment allows. On a barbell, that is usually 2.5 kg or 5 lb total, or less if your gym has micro plates. On dumbbells, it is often 2 to 2.5 kg or 5 lb per hand. On machines, move one pin. Smaller jumps keep your reps inside the range and make progress more consistent over time.

### Is double progression good for beginners?

Yes, although many complete beginners can simply add weight every session for the first few months. Double progression becomes most useful once those simple weight increases start causing missed reps. It is also a good choice for beginners training with dumbbells or machines, where weight jumps are large. The rules are simple, which makes it easy to follow from a basic training log.

### What should I do if I stall on double progression?

First check sleep, food, rest between sets, and whether your reps are clean. If those are fine, try a deload week, widen your rep range, add a set before increasing weight, or move one set to the heavier weight at a time. If a lift has not progressed in three or four weeks despite these changes, try a different variation or rep range for a training block.
