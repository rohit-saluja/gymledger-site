How many sets per muscle group per week? For most people building muscle, about 10 to 20 hard sets. Beginners grow well on 6 to 10, most intermediates do well on 10 to 16, and advanced lifters can push a priority muscle toward 20 or a little beyond. Count a set only when it ends within about three reps of failure, count compound lifts as half a set for the helper muscles, and spread the total over at least two sessions, with no more than about 10 sets for one muscle in a single workout.

The rest of this guide shows where those numbers come from, how to count sets without a spreadsheet, how different splits share them out, and when adding sets helps or just makes you tired. It sits in the same cluster as our guide on [how to track progressive overload](/blog/how-to-track-progressive-overload). Weekly sets are the dose, and your log tells you whether the dose is working.

## How many sets per muscle group per week, by training level

| Level | Weekly hard sets per muscle | Sessions per muscle per week | Example for chest |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Beginner** (first year) | 6 to 10 | 2 | Two full-body days x 4 sets = 8 |
| **Intermediate** (1 to 4 years) | 10 to 16 | 2 | Two upper days x 6 sets = 12 |
| **Advanced** (4+ years) | 12 to 20, up to about 25 for one priority muscle for a few weeks | 2 to 3 | Three sessions x 6 sets = 18 |

Treat these as starting points, not targets to max out. Beginners grow from almost anything, so piling on sets mostly adds soreness. Further along, you need more work to keep growing, but the extra return from each set gets smaller.

## What the research says about weekly volume

The research on how many sets per muscle group per week you need is better than on most gym topics, though it still mostly comes from young men in short studies.

- **More sets, more growth, up to a point.** A 2017 meta-analysis of 15 studies found a [graded dose-response between weekly sets and muscle growth](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/): each extra weekly set was linked to slightly more growth.
- **12 to 20 sets for trained lifters.** A 2022 systematic review of trained young men concluded that [12 to 20 weekly sets per muscle](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35291645/) may be the best standard recommendation. Going above 20 didn't help the quads or biceps, though it did help the triceps.
- **Diminishing returns.** The biggest analysis so far, 67 studies and 2,058 participants, found [gains in size and strength keep rising with volume but flatten out](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/). Strength flattened sooner than size.
- **The official floor.** The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found that [muscle growth was enhanced by 10 or more sets per week](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/). An earlier umbrella review of 14 meta-analyses landed on the same figure of [at least 10 sets per week per muscle group](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35873210/).

So once you're past the beginner stage, around 10 hard sets per muscle per week is a sensible floor. Somewhere around 20, each extra set buys less and costs more recovery.

If your main goal is strength rather than size, you need fewer sets. The same ACSM review found strength responded best to heavy loads (80 percent of your max and up), a full range of motion, 2 to 3 sets, lifting early in the session, and training at least twice a week.

## How to count sets: direct, indirect and fractional

Before you can answer how many sets you're doing, you need two rules: which sets count, and which muscles they count for.

*Photo: [Alesia Kozik](https://www.pexels.com/photo/a-set-of-dumbbells-and-a-paper-on-yellow-background-8154267/) on Pexels*

### Which sets count

Count **hard sets**: working sets that end within about 0 to 3 reps of failure. On the [RPE chart](/blog/rpe-chart), that's roughly RPE 7 to 10. Warm-ups don't count, and neither does an easy set where you stopped with six reps left.

This matters more than people think. Analyses that estimate reps in reserve suggest [muscle growth improves as sets are taken closer to failure](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/). Fifteen easy sets can do less than ten hard ones.

### Which muscles they count for

A bench press trains your chest, but your triceps and front delts work too. The cleanest way to count it is the **fractional method**: one set for the main muscle, half a set for each helper. In the 67-study analysis above, counting indirect sets as half a set predicted results better than counting them fully or not at all.

| Exercise | Counts as 1 set for | Counts as 0.5 set for |
|---|---|---|
| Bench press, push-up, dip | Chest | Triceps, front delts |
| Overhead press | Front delts | Triceps, side delts |
| Row (barbell, cable, dumbbell) | Upper back and lats | Biceps, rear delts |
| Pull-up, lat pulldown | Lats | Biceps, upper back |
| Squat, leg press | Quads | Glutes |
| Romanian deadlift | Hamstrings | Glutes, lower back |
| Hip thrust | Glutes | Hamstrings |
| Lateral raise, curl, pushdown | The target muscle | Nothing |

### A worked count for one push day

Say your push day is bench press 3 sets, incline dumbbell press 3, overhead press 3, lateral raise 3 and triceps pushdown 3.

| Muscle | Direct sets | Indirect sets (x 0.5) | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chest | 6 (bench 3, incline 3) | 0 | 6 |
| Front delts | 3 (overhead press) | 6 x 0.5 = 3 (bench, incline) | 6 |
| Side delts | 3 (lateral raise) | 3 x 0.5 = 1.5 (overhead press) | 4.5 |
| Triceps | 3 (pushdown) | 9 x 0.5 = 4.5 (all three presses) | 7.5 |

Two things jump out. Your triceps get more work than you'd guess from one isolation exercise. And your front delts get plenty from pressing, which is why most programs skip front raises.

## How many sets per muscle in one workout

Weekly totals are only half of it. Twenty chest sets on Monday is not the same as ten on Monday and ten on Thursday.

RP Strength calls this the [11-set rule](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/podcasts/training-frequency-decoded-the-11-set-rule-every-lifter-should-know): past about 10 to 11 hard sets for one muscle in one session, extra sets add little, and they're better moved to another day. It's a coaching rule of thumb rather than a hard research finding, but it matches what most lifters see in their logs. By set 12 of chest, you're doing fewer reps with worse form and calling it volume.

Frequency is mostly a way to fit your sets in. A meta-analysis of 25 studies found that [training a muscle more often made no meaningful difference to growth when weekly sets were equal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30558493/). The 67-study analysis did find that strength rose with frequency. So more sessions per week let each set be a good set, and help strength a little on top.

| Weekly sets for a muscle | Sessions per week | Sets per session |
|---|---|---|
| 6 to 10 | 1 to 2 | 4 to 6 |
| 10 to 16 | 2 | 5 to 8 |
| 16 to 20 | 2 to 3 | 6 to 10 |
| 20+ | 3 or more | 6 to 10 |

## Rep ranges for hypertrophy vs strength

Sets and reps get asked about together, so here's the rep side. The best muscle-building rep range is wider than gym folklore says.

A 2017 meta-analysis of 21 studies, with every set taken to failure, found [muscle growth was similar with light and heavy loads, but max strength improved more with heavy loads](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/). Helms and colleagues, writing on [effort-based training](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4961270/), suggest working mostly in the 6 to 12 range. That range builds muscle about as well as heavier work and takes less time per session.

| Goal | Reps per set | How hard | Example (kg) | Example (lb) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Strength** (main lifts) | 3 to 6 | 1 to 3 reps left | Bench 85 x 5 | Bench 185 x 5 |
| **Size**, compound lifts | 6 to 12 | 1 to 3 reps left | Bench 70 x 10 | Bench 155 x 10 |
| **Size**, isolation | 10 to 20, up to 30 | 0 to 2 reps left | Lateral raise 8 x 15 | Lateral raise 15 x 15 |

For counting purposes, a hard set of 6 and a hard set of 15 both count as one set for growth. For strength, the heavy sets matter more.

## Weekly sets per muscle: a starting table

Here's a per-muscle plan for an intermediate lifter. The research mostly measured chest, quads, biceps and triceps, so treat the smaller muscles as coaching starting points rather than study results. Direct sets are listed; the third column shows the indirect work each muscle already gets.

| Muscle | Direct sets to start | Typical range | Already gets indirect work from | Example week (two sessions) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Chest** | 10 | 8 to 16 | Nothing major | Bench 3 + 3, incline DB 2 + 2 |
| **Back (lats, upper back)** | 12 | 10 to 20 | Deadlifts | Row 3 + 3, pulldown 3 + 3 |
| **Quads** | 10 | 8 to 16 | Lunges | Squat 3 + 3, leg press 2 + 2 |
| **Hamstrings** | 6 | 6 to 12 | Deadlifts | RDL 3, leg curl 3 |
| **Glutes** | 4 | 0 to 10 | Squats, lunges, RDLs | Hip thrust 2 + 2 |
| **Side delts** | 8 | 6 to 16 | Overhead press | Lateral raise 4 + 4 |
| **Rear delts** | 4 | 4 to 10 | Rows, pulldowns | Reverse fly 2 + 2 |
| **Front delts** | 0 | 0 to 6 | All pressing | None needed |
| **Biceps** | 6 | 6 to 12 | Rows, pulldowns | Curl 3 + 3 |
| **Triceps** | 6 | 6 to 12 | All pressing | Pushdown 3 + 3 |
| **Calves** | 8 | 6 to 12 | Very little | Calf raise 4 + 4 |
| **Abs** | 6 | 4 to 10 | Squats, deadlifts | Cable crunch 3 + 3 |

Run the starting column for four to six weeks before you change anything. If a muscle is clearly lagging, move it up the range first. Don't raise everything at once.

## How workout splits distribute the sets

Your split decides how many times a week you can hit each muscle, and that decides how many sets fit before you run into the per-session cap. Here's chest across five common setups.

| Split | Chest sessions per week | Chest sets per session | Weekly chest sets |
|---|---|---|---|
| [3-day full body](/blog/3-day-workout-split) | 3 | 4 | 12 |
| [4-day upper/lower](/blog/4-day-workout-split) | 2 | 6 | 12 |
| [Push/pull/legs](/blog/push-pull-legs-routine), 6 days | 2 | 8 | 16 |
| Push/pull/legs, 3 days | 1 | 10 | 10 |
| Bro split, one chest day | 1 | 12 to 16 | 12 to 16, the last few past the cap |

The full-body and upper/lower splits reach the 10 to 16 range comfortably with short, high-quality sessions. A six-day push/pull/legs routine gives you room to go higher. A once-a-week chest day can hit the same weekly number on paper, but the last third of those sets is usually done tired.

## When to add sets, and when not to

Extra sets are a fix for a stall, so don't add them by default. Add one or two sets per week to a muscle only when all three of these are true:

1. **Its lifts have been flat for three or more weeks**, and sleep, food and rest between sets are in order.
2. **You're recovering well.** Reps hold from session to session, and you're not still sore when that muscle comes round again.
3. **You're below about 20 weekly sets** for that muscle.

There's some support for adding sets gradually. In a 12-week study of trained men, [adding sets to leg training every two weeks](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37796222/) produced bigger squat strength gains than keeping sets constant. Muscle-size differences were less certain and seemed to level off at the higher volumes.

### Worked example: a chest volume block

| Week | Weekly chest sets | Bench, first 3 sets (kg) | Bench, first 3 sets (lb) | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 | 70 x 8, 8, 7 | 155 x 8, 8, 7 | Baseline |
| 2 | 12 | 70 x 8, 8, 8 | 155 x 8, 8, 8 | Reps up: good |
| 3 | 14 | 72.5 x 8, 7, 7 | 160 x 8, 7, 7 | Load up: good |
| 4 | 16 | 72.5 x 7, 7, 6 | 160 x 7, 7, 6 | Reps falling: stop adding |
| 5 | 8 | 65 x 8, 8 | 145 x 8, 8 | Easy week |
| 6 | 12 to 14 | 72.5 x 8, 8, 7 | 160 x 8, 8, 7 | Restart at what worked |

In this lifter's log, 12 to 14 sets worked and 16 was too many. Week 5 is a lighter week to shed fatigue (see our [deload week guide](/blog/deload-week)), and week 6 goes back to the volume that worked, not the one that didn't.

GymLedger shows your weekly volume on the dashboard and a strength chart for every exercise. If weekly volume keeps climbing while the chart for your main chest lift goes flat, the extra sets aren't paying for themselves.

## Junk volume: sets that don't count

Junk volume is any set that costs recovery but adds little growth. The usual suspects:

- **Sets far from failure.** Stopping at RPE 5 with five or six reps left. Comfortable, but closer to a warm-up than a working set.
- **Sets after performance collapses.** Bench at 70 kg (155 lb) going 8, 8, 7, 6, 4, 3. The last two sets add 7 reps at a lot of fatigue. Stop after set 4 and move the work to another day.
- **Sets past the per-session cap.** Set 14 of chest in one workout.
- **Sloppy reps.** Half-range reps and heavy swinging inflate the count without adding much.
- **Sets that clash with recovery.** Hard quad work the day after heavy squats, when your quads still can't produce their normal reps.

A practical rule of thumb: when your reps on an exercise drop by about a third from your first set at the same weight, you've got what you need from it today.

## Putting it into practice

1. **Pick your level's range**: 6 to 10 sets per muscle per week as a beginner, 10 to 16 as an intermediate, 12 to 20 if you're advanced.
2. **Count only hard sets**, ending with about 0 to 3 reps left. Count compound lifts as half a set for the helper muscles.
3. **Split each muscle's sets over at least two sessions**, with about 10 hard sets at most per session.
4. **Train mostly in the 6 to 20 rep range**, with heavier 3 to 6 rep sets on main lifts if strength matters to you.
5. **Run the per-muscle table for four to six weeks**, then add one or two sets only where a muscle has stalled and you're recovering well.
6. **Cut junk sets** before adding new ones.

Set it up once in your workout templates, with exercises, sets and rep ranges, and the weekly count takes care of itself. Then your log and charts will tell you whether the number is right for you.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many sets per muscle group per week for hypertrophy?

Most people build muscle well with about 10 to 20 hard sets per muscle group per week. Beginners can start with 6 to 10 and still grow quickly. Intermediates usually do well with 10 to 16, and advanced lifters may push one or two priority muscles toward 20 for a training block. Count only sets taken within about three reps of failure, and spread them across at least two sessions.

### Is 20 sets per muscle per week too much?

Not necessarily, but it is near the point where returns shrink. Research on trained lifters found 12 to 20 weekly sets worked well, and going above 20 helped some muscles but not others. Twenty sets makes sense for an advanced lifter's priority muscle, split over two or three sessions. For a beginner, or for every muscle at once, it is usually more fatigue than growth.

### How many sets per muscle should I do in one workout?

A common coaching rule is to cap it at about 10 hard sets for one muscle in a single session. Beyond that, reps and form tend to drop and the extra sets add little. If you want more weekly volume for a muscle, add another session for it instead of making one workout longer. Two sessions of 6 to 8 sets usually beat one session of 14.

### Do compound exercises count toward every muscle they work?

Partly. A practical method is to count one full set for the main muscle and half a set for each helper. A set of bench press counts as one set for chest and half a set each for triceps and front delts. Research comparing counting methods found this fractional approach predicted muscle growth better than counting helper muscles fully or ignoring them.

### What is the best muscle building rep range?

Muscle grows across a wide range of reps, roughly 6 to 30, as long as sets end close to failure. Most lifters do best spending most of their time between 6 and 12 reps on compound lifts and 10 to 20 on isolation exercises, because very heavy sets take longer and very light sets are uncomfortable. For maximal strength, include heavier sets of 3 to 6 reps on your main lifts.

### Do warm-up sets count toward weekly sets?

No. Weekly set targets refer to hard working sets that end within about three reps of failure. Warm-up sets prepare you for those sets but are too easy to count toward growth. Log them separately, or not at all, so they do not inflate your weekly total. The same goes for very easy back-off sets where you stop with many reps left.
